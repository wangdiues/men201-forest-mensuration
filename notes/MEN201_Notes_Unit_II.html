Section 1Learning outcomes

  1. Use units correctly — convert length, area and volume without mixing dimensions.
  2. Relate girth and diameter — move confidently between C, d and r.
  3. Choose and use instruments — match scale, calliper and tape to the tree and the required accuracy.
  4. Locate breast height — apply the protocol reference height on slopes, leaning stems, forks and buttresses.
  5. Measure bark thickness — sample non-uniform bark with a gauge or probe.
  6. Convert over bark to under bark — calculate Dub and Gub with clear notation.
Why this is the most consequential unit in the module

Diameter enters basal area as a square and volume as roughly a square. A 1 % error here is about 2 % in every basal area, volume, biomass and carbon figure downstream — and nothing later in the module can repair it.

Section 2Key terms and notation

The module uses one notation system throughout. Lower case is one tree; upper case is the stand.

Diameter at breast height (DBH)
The diameter of the stem measured at the reference height the governing protocol prescribes. Recorded over bark unless stated otherwise.
Over bark (o.b.) / under bark (u.b.)
Whether the bark is included in the dimension. This is part of the variable, not an afterthought — o.b. and u.b. diameters are different quantities.
Circular-equivalent diameter
The diameter a perfect circle of the measured circumference would have, d = C/π. What a girth tape reports, and what a diameter tape's printed scale gives directly.
Basal area
The cross-sectional area of the stem at breast height. For one tree, g = πd²/4.

Section 3Girth, diameter and basal area

C = 2πr = πd   ·   d = C / π true only for a circular cross-section

Since 1/π ≈ 0.3183, a girth tape reading multiplied by 0.3183 gives the circular-equivalent diameter. A diameter tape does this for you: its scale is printed with each diameter unit spaced π units apart on the physical tape, so 3.14159 cm of tape reads as 1.00 cm of diameter.

Basal area

g = πd² / 4 = πr² with d in metres, g is in m²

Foresters normally measure diameter in centimetres but want basal area in square metres. Combining the unit conversion with π/4 gives the constant used throughout Units II, V and VI:

g (m²) = 0.00007854 × d²  (d in cm)

Because 0.00007854 = π/40000, this is exactly πd²/4 with d converted from cm to m. Learn the constant, but know where it comes from — it is examinable either way.

Section 4Instruments and their errors

No instrument is best in every field situation. Choose by: standing or felled tree; position of the log; required accuracy; diameter range; terrain and access.

InstrumentReadsStrengthsError signature

CalliperdiameterDirect reading, quick, little calculation, usable by trained assistantsMisadjusted or out-of-square jaws; awkward on large stems; two axes hard on steep ground
Girth tapecircumferenceCompact, one encircling measurement, works round felled logsPositive bias on rough bark; tension varies between operators; hard to keep level
Diameter tapediameterDirect reading, shorter scale, convenient for repeated inventorySame as girth tape, plus it assumes a perfect circle
Relascopeangle → diameterUpper-stem diameters out of reach; no contact errorNeeds clear sight, accurate distance, a practised observer. Not part of the revised module and not assessed
Why a tape reads high

A tape follows the convex hull of the bark: it bridges the fissures instead of following them in and out. The encircled path is therefore longer than the true perimeter of the wood, and the circular-equivalent diameter comes out too large. The bias is one-directional, so it never averages out — report it, do not call it an error.

Species factors — handle with care

A real stem is not a circle, so the ratio d/C is not exactly 1/π. Published species factors exist (chir pine 0.3093, sal 0.3119, teak 0.3160, against 0.3183 for a perfect circle).

When to use one

Only where the inventory manual names the species, region and derivation. Bhutan's National Forest Inventory does not apply species d/C factors — it records the tape reading as circular-equivalent diameter. Otherwise you have introduced a correction nobody else can reproduce.

Section 5Breast height and the protocol

Breast height is a convention, not a law of nature. It was chosen because it is:

  • a convenient working height, with less fatigue over a long day;
  • usually above the undergrowth and the basal swelling;
  • usually above buttress or abnormal basal growth;
  • independent of an irregular stump height after felling.
The value itself is protocol-dependent

Bhutan's Second National Forest Inventory recorded over-bark DBH at 1.37 m. Many international manuals use 1.30 m; the American 4.5 ft is also 1.37 m. Neither is universally correct.

The rule: identify and follow the applicable inventory protocol, and record the reference height you used. Comparability matters more than personal convenience.

The Second NFI protocol in full

Reference height1.37 m, over bark
Treesfrom 10 cm DBH upwards
Saplings5 cm to under 10 cm DBH
Plotthree plots of 12.62 m radius per cluster — 500 m² each
Grid4 × 4 km national sampling grid
Captureelectronic, Open Foris Collect Mobile
Reportingestimates published with 95 % confidence intervals
A 9.8 cm stem is not a small tree

It is a sapling, and it does not enter the tree tally. A crew that rounds it up to 10 cm has not been generous — it has manufactured a tree, and the error propagates into stems per hectare, basal area and every volume built on them.

One country, several protocols

National inventory, FMU inventory, community-forest inventory and teaching exercises may each prescribe something different. Read the field manual before you touch a tape — do not assume that what you learned in a lecture is what this job requires.

Section 6Awkward stems

SituationRule
Sloping groundMeasure the reference height from the uphill side
Leaning stemMeasure the reference height along the stem, not vertically
Buttressed or flutedMeasure just above the buttress, at a point where it no longer influences the stem, and record that height
Forked below breast heightTreat as two trees
Forked above breast heightTreat as one tree
Swelling or wound at breast heightFollow the applicable inventory protocol. Record the abnormality, the measurement positions and the calculation method
The two rules for an abnormality are not interchangeable

Some protocols prescribe measuring immediately above or below the abnormality; others prescribe measuring above and below and taking the mean. They give different numbers. Do not treat them as universal alternatives — find out which one governs your job, apply it, and record what you did.

Why the uphill rule is not a nicety

Bhutan is mountain forestry. If half a crew measures uphill and half downhill, the difference is systematic, not random — it does not average out over the plot, and it biases every crop parameter derived from it.

Before measuring

  1. Inspect — slope, lean, fork, buttress, abnormalities.
  2. Locate — set the protocol reference height from the correct ground reference.
  3. Prepare — remove moss, creepers and lichen; remove loose bark without cutting sound bark; exclude vines from under the tape.
  4. Measure — calibrated instrument, correct orientation, tape flat and perpendicular.
  5. Repeat — the specified second axis or the bark readings.
  6. Record — value, unit, o.b./u.b., height and exception code. Mark the point with a cross for permanent plots.

Section 7Bark thickness

Bark thickness is the distance from the outer surface to the cambium boundary. It is not a fixed correction; it varies at four scales — among species, within a species, among trees with age and locality, and along a single stem (usually thicker near the base).

Sampling

  1. Align with the first diameter axis; measure bark at both opposite endpoints.
  2. Rotate 90° and measure the second opposite pair.
  3. Average explicitly: t̄ = (t₁ + t₂ + t₃ + t₄) / 4

The conversions

Dub = Dob − 2t̄ a diameter crosses the bark twice — subtract 2t̄, never t̄
Gub = Gob − 2πt̄ reducing the radius by t reduces the circumference by 2πt
Worked example 2 · one tree, two boundaries

Given: Dob = 40.0 cm, Gob = 125.7 cm, mean one-sided bark t̄ = 1.5 cm.

Double barkB = 2 × 1.5 = 3.0 cm
Diameter under barkDub = 40.0 − 3.0 = 37.0 cm
Girth under barkGub = 125.7 − 2π(1.5) = 125.7 − 9.42 = 116.3 cm
Cross-checkπ × 37.0 = 116.2 cm ✓
Dub = 37.0 cm · Gub ≈ 116.3 cm

Always take the second route. When two independent routes to the same number agree to a millimetre, both are probably right. The 0.1 cm difference here is rounding in Gob.

Bark as a percentage

bark % of diameter = (2t̄ / Dob) × 100   ·   bark % of area = [1 − (Dub/Dob)²] × 100

For the tree above: 3.0/40.0 = 7.5 % of the diameter, but 1 − (37/40)² = 14.4 % of the area. Bark is a ring, not a slab. Stripping a thin outer ring removes a disproportionate share of the cross-section, because area follows d².

Section 8Calibration and repeatability

Calibrate at the start of every field season and after any drop.

  • Girth and diameter tapes stretch with use. Check against a steel rule over a known 1 m span; retire a tape more than 2 mm out.
  • Steel tapes do not stretch appreciably but kink, and the zero end wears.
  • Callipers go out of square. Check the movable arm reads zero closed and stays parallel across the beam.
  • Bark gauges — check the scale zero against the cutting edge.
Why calibration and not more measurements

An instrument error is systematic. It has the same sign on every tree, so it survives averaging intact. Measuring twice as many trees with a stretched tape gives a more precise wrong answer. Only calibration removes it. This is Unit I's distinction, met for the first time on a real instrument.

Working tolerances for this module

DBH, blind re-measurewithin 0.3 cm
Girth, blind re-measurewithin 1.0 cm
Bark thickness, one sidewithin 2 mm
Reference-height markwithin 2 cm

These are teaching values. For real work the binding tolerances come from the field manual governing that inventory.

Blind means blind. Student A measures, records, and covers the sheet. Student B measures independently. Only then compare. If B can see A's number the exercise measures nothing except social pressure. Re-measure 5–10 % of trees this way; the re-measurement data are what allow error to be quantified rather than guessed.

Outside tolerance? Do not average and move on. Find the cause: usually the mark has moved, the tape is twisted, or the two are measuring different axes on an oval stem.

Section 9In Bhutanese forestry

Diameter is the most-measured quantity in Bhutanese forestry and the first measurement in every chain that follows.

  • Community-forest inventory. A group measures its own stems, usually with a girth tape, to prepare and revise its management plan. The numbers decide how much timber it may take each year.
  • Timber marking. Trees marked for felling are selected and scaled on diameter; the rural timber allotment that follows is a volume derived from it.
  • NFI tree measurement. Every tree of 10 cm DBH and above in every plot of the national grid — the raw material of the State of Forest Report.
  • Biomass and carbon. The Bhutan allometric equations of Unit VI take basal area as their predictor. Diameter is where a national carbon figure begins.
Beyond timber

The same tape readings describe diameter-class diversity, large habitat trees, deadwood, regeneration and change between inventories. Diameter is not a timber measurement; it is a forest-structure measurement that timber happens to use.

Section 10Common errors

Most mistakes here are not arithmetic. They are errors of placement and definition, and they are invisible in the data.

Placement

  • Measuring from the downhill side on a slope.
  • Tape tilted rather than perpendicular — it then measures an ellipse, which is always longer.
  • Calliper axis not representative of an oval stem.
  • Measuring directly on a swelling or a fork.
  • Including vines or loose bark under the tape.
  • Measuring at your own chest height rather than the prescribed reference height. Issue a marked pole cut to that height.

Definition and recording

  • Confusing C with d.
  • Omitting units.
  • Not stating over bark or under bark.
  • Subtracting t̄ only once from Dob.
  • Failing to record a shifted measurement height.
  • Applying a species d/C factor the inventory does not use.

Section 11Field practical

Use the printed field sheet.

  1. Mark the reference height and photograph or sketch the terrain case.
  2. Measure two perpendicular diameters with a calliper.
  3. Measure girth with a tape and convert to circular-equivalent diameter.
  4. Take four bark readings at the two diameter axes.
  5. Calculate Dub and Gub, showing units and definitions.
  6. Blind re-measure 5–10 % of the trees and complete the QC block in the field.
  7. Write a three-sentence error diagnosis: geometry, instrument, operator.

Section 12Practice questions

  1. Why is breast height measured from the uphill side on a slope?
  2. Why does rough bark usually produce a positive tape bias? Is the bias random or systematic?
  3. When is a fork counted as one tree, and when as two?
  4. A tree has C = 94.2 cm. Estimate its circular-equivalent diameter.
  5. Why must bark be sampled at opposite points, and why four readings rather than one?
  6. Given Dob = 52 cm and t̄ = 1.8 cm, calculate Dub and the bark percentage of area.
  7. A stem is 42.0 cm o.b. with a mean one-sided bark of 1.1 cm. Compute g over bark and g under bark in m², and express the difference as a percentage.
  8. State Bhutan's Second NFI DBH protocol in full — reference height, tree and sapling thresholds, plot radius and grid.
  9. Two crews measure the same 40-tree plot. Crew A's tape is 3 mm long over 1 m; Crew B's readings scatter by ±0.4 cm with no bias. Which crew's basal area total is more trustworthy, and why?

Worked solutions to questions 4, 6, 7 and 9 are in the calculation handbook.

Section 13Summary

  • Keep dimensions and units consistent. g (m²) = 0.00007854 d² with d in cm.
  • C = πd holds only for a circular cross-section.
  • Know the instrument's error profile. A tape is biased high on rough bark.
  • Measure at a standard, recorded point. The reference height is protocol-dependent — 1.37 m in Bhutan's Second NFI, 1.30 m in many international manuals.
  • State over bark or under bark. Dub = Dob − 2t̄, Gub = Gob − 2πt̄.
  • Calibrate rather than measure more: instrument error is systematic and survives averaging.
  • Before writing a number, write its unit, reference height and bark boundary.
