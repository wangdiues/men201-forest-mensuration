Crown terms — four names, no ambiguity

  • Crown-base height — ground to the base of the live crown.
  • Crown length — total height minus crown-base height.
  • Crown midpoint height — ground to the midpoint of the live crown.
  • Live-crown ratio — crown length ÷ total height.

The bare term “crown height” is not used: it carries three incompatible meanings in the literature.

Section 4Derivation · the four solids

Take the cross-sectional area at distance x from the tip as S = k xr. The volume of the full solid of length l is

V = ∫₀l k xr dx = k lr+1 / (r+1)

The basal area at the base is Sb = k lr, so V = Sb l / (r+1). That single line gives the whole middle column of the master table:

Solidrr+1Volume
Cylinder01S l
Paraboloid12S l / 2
Cone23S l / 3
Neiloid34S l / 4

Why Newton's formula is exact for all four

Newton's is Simpson's rule applied to the area function along the log:

V = ∫₀l S(x) dx ≈ (l/6)[S₁ + 4Sm + S₂]

Simpson's rule is exact for any polynomial of degree ≤ 3. The four solids have area functions of degree 0, 1, 2 and 3 in x — so all four are integrated exactly. Smalian's is the trapezoidal rule, exact only to degree 1; Huber's is the midpoint rule, also exact only to degree 1.

Why Huber's error is about half Smalian's, with opposite sign

For a smooth function the trapezoidal rule errs by +(l³/12)S″ and the midpoint rule by −(l³/24)S″. The midpoint error is therefore half the size and opposite in sign. Since a real log is convex (S″ > 0), Smalian over-estimates and Huber under-estimates — exactly what Worked example 4 in Unit V shows.

Section 5Derivation · the quarter-girth formula

For a circular section of circumference C, the radius is r = C/2π and the area is

S = πr² = π (C/2π)² = C² / 4π  →  V = C² l / 4π

The Hoppus convention replaces π with 4:

QGV = C² l / (4 × 4) = C² l / 16 = (C/4)² l

The ratio is therefore fixed and exact:

QGV / V = (C² l / 16) ÷ (C² l / 4π) = 4π/16 = π/4 = 0.785398

So quarter-girth measure records 78.54 % of the true volume — a systematic 21.46 % under-measurement, independent of log size.

Section 6Derivation · the spacing factor

  1. On a square lattice of spacing s metres, each tree occupies s² m², so N = 10 000 / s² trees per hectare.
  2. By definition s = F d, with d in metres, so N = 10 000 / (F² d²).
  3. Basal area per hectare is G = N × πd²/4.
  4. Substituting, the d² cancels: G = (10 000 / (F² d²)) × πd²/4 = 10 000π / (4F²).
G = 7 853.98 / F² ≈ 7 854 / F²
The surprise, and the warning

G depends only on the relative spacing, not on tree size at all. But F sits in the denominator squared, so a pace 5 % short reports a basal area about 10 % high. Calibrate your pace.

Section 7Derivation · the six sine cases

Let A be the tip, B the base, E the eye, and θ the angle of lean. Apply the sine rule to triangle AEB:

AB / sin ∠AEB = EB / sin ∠EAB  →  AB = EB · sin ∠AEB ÷ sin ∠EAB

The numerator ∠AEB is just the angle subtended at the eye, and depends only on where you stand:

PositionAngles read∠AEB
a · between base and topα elevation, β depressionα + β
b · below base and topboth elevationsα − β
c · above base and topboth depressionsβ − α

Finding ∠EAB — case 2a, leaning away

Drop C as the point where the vertical through B meets the sight line EA. In triangle ABC the exterior angle at C equals the sum of the two interior opposite angles:

  1. ∠ECB = 90 − α  (the vertical BC against the sight line)
  2. Exterior angle: ∠ECB = ∠CAB + ∠CBA
  3. Substitute, with ∠CBA = θ:   90 − α = ∠CAB + θ
  4. Solve:   ∠CAB = 90 − (α + θ) = ∠EAB
  5. Since sin(90 − x) = cos x:   sin ∠EAB = cos (α + θ)
AB = EB · sin(α + β) ÷ cos(α + θ)

Leaning towards the observer reverses the sign of θ, giving cos(α − θ). The same construction gives every other case; the numerator changes with position and the sign inside the cosine with lean direction.

CaseLeaning towardsLeaning away
a · betweenEB sin(α+β) / cos(α−θ)EB sin(α+β) / cos(α+θ)
b · belowEB sin(α−β) / cos(α−θ)EB sin(α−β) / cos(α+θ)
c · aboveEB sin(β−α) / cos(α+θ)EB sin(β−α) / cos(α−θ)

The physical check: a bigger denominator gives a smaller height, which matches the fact that a tree leaning away is further off than it looks.

Section 8Derivation · why the m.a.i culminates where the c.a.i crosses it

Without calculus

The m.a.i at age a is the average of all the annual increments up to a. Adding one more year at the c.a.i for that year:

  • if the new value is above the running average, the average rises;
  • if it is below, the average falls;
  • if it is equal, the average is unchanged — it is stationary.

A quantity that rises while c.a.i > m.a.i and falls while c.a.i < m.a.i must reach its maximum exactly where c.a.i = m.a.i.

With calculus

Let V(a) be cumulative volume. Then m.a.i = V/a and c.a.i = dV/da.

d/da (V/a) = (a · dV/da − V) / a²

Setting the derivative to zero: a · dV/da = V, that is dV/da = V/a — c.a.i equals m.a.i. ∎

The generalisation worth naming

Any running average peaks where the current value crosses it. The age of maximum average annual production is necessarily the age at which current production falls to the average. This is not a fact about forests; it is a fact about averages.

Section 9Evaluating the restricted cubic spline

Both the Bhutan volume models and the Bhutan biomass equations use the same spline term. With knots t₁, t₂, t₃ and predictor x:

X₂ = g(x) = (x−t₁)³₊ − (x−t₂)³₊ · (t₃−t₁)/(t₃−t₂) + (x−t₃)³₊ · (t₂−t₁)/(t₃−t₂)

where ( )₊ means: cube it only if the bracket is positive; otherwise the term is zero.

Step by step

  1. Compute the predictor — BA in m², or BAH in m³. Never DBH in cm.
  2. Compute the three brackets x−t₁, x−t₂, x−t₃.
  3. Any bracket that is negative or zero contributes nothing. Cross it out.
  4. Cube each surviving bracket.
  5. Multiply the second by (t₃−t₁)/(t₃−t₂) and the third by (t₂−t₁)/(t₃−t₂).
  6. Combine with the signs shown: + first, − second, + third.
Worked · the study's validation tree, Model 16

DBH 54.1 cm, height 23.4 m. Knots 0.193, 0.871, 9.294.

BAπ × 0.541² / 4 = 0.229871 m²
BAH0.229871 × 23.4 = 5.37898 m³
x − t₁5.37898 − 0.193 = 5.18598 → 5.18598³ = 139.4738
x − t₂5.37898 − 0.871 = 4.50798 → 4.50798³ = 91.6107
x − t₃5.37898 − 9.294 = negative → 0
Multiplier(9.294 − 0.193)/(9.294 − 0.871) = 9.101/8.423 = 1.08049
X₂139.4738 − 91.6107 × 1.08049 = 139.4738 − 98.9844 = 40.4894
V−0.0009352 + 0.4284381(5.37898) − 0.0016409(40.4894)
V = 2.237 m³  (field measured 2.109 m³, error +6.1 %)
The two things people get wrong

Forgetting that the third bracket is zero when the predictor lies below the outer knot; and cubing before subtracting instead of after. Both give a plausible-looking wrong answer.

SolutionsUnit I

Q3 · why a 1 % diameter error does not stay 1 %

Basal area follows d², and volume follows roughly d². Take g = k d². If d becomes d(1+ε), then g → k d²(1+ε)² = g(1 + 2ε + ε²). For small ε the ε² term is negligible, so the relative error in g is approximately 2ε — double the relative error in d. A 1 % diameter error is about 2 % in basal area; a 5 % error is about 10 %.

Q5 · justifying “absolute accuracy is neither necessary nor desirable”

A complete answer names and explains at least four of the six reasons — tree characteristics (no tree is a true solid of revolution, so every formula assumes a shape the tree does not have); varying felling and conversion practice (stump height and crosscutting change the volume recorded from the same tree); instruments and the conditions of their use; the biological character of the forest (it grows while you measure it and may be destroyed before the plan is executed); the use to which the figure is put (a thinning reconnaissance does not need the accuracy of a sale); and cost (accuracy is bought with time and people, and doubling precision costs about four times the effort). The conclusion is that the professional question is not “how accurate can I be?” but “how accurate does this job need to be, and what will that cost?”

Q6 · classifying five readings of a 40.0 cm stem

Readings 43.1, 43.0, 43.2, 43.1, 43.0 cm. The spread is 0.2 cm, so the set is highly precise. The mean is 43.08 cm against a true 40.0 cm, so it is inaccurate by about 3.1 cm — a systematic error of roughly 7.7 %. This is the dangerous quadrant: the data look excellent. More readings will not help. Check the instrument for stretch or a worn zero, check the measuring point, and re-measure against a known standard.

Q9 · which crew's basal area total is more trustworthy

Crew B. Crew A's tape is 3 mm long over 1 m — a systematic +0.3 % on every girth, which becomes about +0.6 % on every basal area, in the same direction on all forty trees. It survives averaging intact and the total is biased high. Crew B's ±0.4 cm scatter is random: across forty trees the errors partly cancel and the total is close to unbiased, though any individual tree is less precise. Crew A's data look tidier and are worth less. The cure for A is calibration, not more measurement.

Q4 · deriving the 2.5 m spacing

Grid area per point = plot area ÷ number of points = 500 ÷ 100 = 5 m². On a square grid the point-to-point distance is the square root of the grid area: √5 = 2.236 m, rounded to 2.5 m. The guideline also caps spacing at 2.5 m and floors it at 1 m; if the arithmetic gives more than 2.5 m, sample plots are established instead.

Q9 · what is wrong with calling it a “13 % error”

The two figures are different quantities. Model 16 predicts merchantable volume to a 10 cm over-bark top with branches excluded; the sectional measurement is the whole stem over bark including the tip above that point. The 0.18 m³ between them is mostly the discarded top — a difference of definition, not error. Only after both are put on the same definition can any residual be called model or measurement error.

Q10 · why the geographic composition matters

214 of the 249 fitting trees came from Thimphu, Paro and Haa. Stem form responds to site, stocking history and exposure, so a blue pine in Bumthang or Trashigang may carry a different form for the same DBH and height. The model would still return a number, and nothing in the arithmetic would signal the problem. Any application outside the western valleys should say so explicitly and treat the estimate as carrying possible regional bias — which, being systematic, does not average out across a stand.

Q11 · substituting for unreachable plots

It has introduced selection bias of unknown size and direction. Steep, remote and cliff-bound ground is not a random subset of the forest: it carries different stocking, species composition and disturbance history. Replacing those plots with easier ground systematically over-samples the accessible part of the forest, and no statistical adjustment can recover what was never visited. The estimate's standard error will look fine and will be meaningless.

What they should have done: record each unreachable plot as visited and unmeasured, with coordinates and the reason; replace it only under a rule written down before fieldwork began; and record that a replacement was used. A documented gap can be adjusted for; an undocumented substitution cannot.

Q3 · age from periodic measurements

Diameters 25, 28 and 31 cm at five-year intervals.

P₁(28 − 25) ÷ (25 × 5) = 3/125 = 0.024000
P₂(31 − 28) ÷ (28 × 5) = 3/140 = 0.021429
log P₁ − log P₂−1.619789 − (−1.669007) = 0.049218
log d₂ − log d₁1.447158 − 1.397940 = 0.049218
S1.000000 exactly
Age in 20001 ÷ (0.024 × 1) = 41.67 years
Age in 2010 ≈ 52 years

Why S = 1. The two periodic increments are equal in absolute terms (3 cm each). Then P₁/P₂ = [Δ/(d₁T)] ÷ [Δ/(d₂T)] = d₂/d₁ identically, so the two logarithms are the same and their ratio is 1 whatever the numbers. For equal absolute increments the age reduces to d₁ divided by the annual diameter increment: 25 ÷ 0.6 = 41.7 years.

Sense check: a 31 cm stem at about 52 years implies roughly 0.6 cm of diameter a year — a defensible rate for a Bhutanese conifer on a moderate site.

Q5 · growth percentage by three formulae

Core 7.2 cm over the outermost 10 rings; present diameter under bark 58 cm.

Diameter growth7.2 × 2 = 14.4 cm  (a core is a radius)
So58 − 14.4 = 43.6 cm
Simple14.4 ÷ (10 × 43.6) × 100 = 3.30 %
Compound[(58/43.6)0.1 − 1] × 100 = 2.90 %
Pressler14.4 ÷ 101.6 × 20 = 2.83 %
simple > compound > Pressler

The ordering, explained. Simple divides by the original 43.6 for all ten years. Compound divides by a base that grows each year, so its denominator is larger on average. Pressler divides by the mean of the two ends, larger still. The gap widens with n: over one or two years the three converge; over ten years simple overstates by about a sixth. For any financial comparison use compound, because it is the only one comparable with an interest rate.

Q7 · proving the m.a.i culminates where the c.a.i crosses it

Either argument earns full marks; both are set out in Section 8. The non-calculus version: a running average rises while the current value exceeds it, falls while the current value is below it, and is unchanged when they are equal — so it is stationary, and therefore at its maximum, exactly at the crossing. The calculus version: with m.a.i = V/a, setting d/da (V/a) = (a·dV/da − V)/a² = 0 gives dV/da = V/a, that is c.a.i = m.a.i. ∎
