Section 1Learning outcomes

  1. Describe the four traditional methods of estimating a standing tree's age, add increment coring, age a felled tree by ring counting, and evaluate each.
  2. Estimate a tree's age from periodic diameter measurements, and state the assumptions.
  3. Define increment, distinguish current annual, periodic annual, total and mean annual increment, and separate live stock from total production.
  4. Locate the culmination of the mean annual increment from a yield series, and state precisely what it does and does not determine.
  5. Compute increment percentage by three formulae, explain why they differ, and say why none of them is a financial return.
  6. Recover a past diameter from an increment core.
The sentence the unit is built on

Age converts a measurement into a rate. Everything in Units II to VI told you how much is there; this unit tells you how fast it is arriving.

Section 2Determining age

Age is harder to know than it looks, and “age” itself needs defining. Biological age runs from germination. Planting age runs from planting out of the nursery. A seeding-felling date is only a proxy for when regeneration began, not the germination year. State which one you mean.

Method 1 · existing records

Plantation journals, working plans, compartment histories. In a documented plantation of one planting year, with beating up recorded, this can be the most reliable evidence of planting age. For natural regeneration it is weak: recruitment can spread over years or decades (the source's illustration is up to 40 years, a local figure). The method that most often turns out to be wrong in a way nobody notices — always cross-check a record against the tree.

Method 2 · general appearance

Bark character, branching habit, crown form. Cannot be examined and cannot be dispensed with: it is the method that tells an experienced forester the record is wrong. Judgement is the cross-check on the arithmetic.

A caution on bark

Bark character must be learned species by species. Do not generalise a bark description from one species to another. Sal, for example, is relatively smooth with a few long vertical cracks when young, and thick, dark, rough and deeply furrowed when old. Treat appearance as a rough local indicator only.

Method 3 · whorls of branches and annual shoots

Many conifers produce exactly one whorl of branches a year from a single terminal bud. Count the whorls, and you have counted the years.

Age = number of whorls + years for the part not visible

The whole difficulty is the second term. The lowest whorls are shed by natural pruning as the crown lifts and their scars grow over, so a ground count always undercounts. The correction estimates the years taken to reach the lowest whorl still visible.

Worked example 1 · whorl count
Whorls counted14
Lowest visible whorl1.2 m above ground
Years to reach 1.2 m≈ 3 (from local experience)
Age ≈ 17 years

Where it fails: broadleaves, which do not whorl; conifers on poor sites that miss a year; and any tree browsed or with a broken leader, which produces a false or doubled whorl.

Method 4 · periodic measurements

The tree is measured three times at equal intervals. No rings are needed, so the method can be used where rings are unreliable. But the age comes from a growth model, not from an observation: three recent diameters do not record germination.

SymbolMeaning
d₁the initial diameter
d₂, d₃diameters at the subsequent periodic measurements
P₁growth per unit diameter per year, first period
P₂growth per unit diameter per year, second period
Tlength of each period, in years
P₁ = (d₂ − d₁) ÷ (d₁ × T)   ·   P₂ = (d₃ − d₂) ÷ (d₂ × T) a proportional growth rate per year — not a percentage
S = (log P₁ − log P₂) ÷ (log d₂ − log d₁)   ·   Age = 1 ÷ (P₁ S)
The idea worth understanding

S measures how fast the proportional growth rate falls as diameter rises. Assume it fell the same way all through the tree's life, and 1/(P₁S) runs that decay backwards to a seedling.

On the logarithms: any base will do provided you use the same base throughout, because the base cancels in a ratio.

When the estimate is meaningful

Only if the intervals are equal, diameters and growth rates are positive, d₂ ≠ d₁ so the denominator is not zero, S > 0, and no suppression or release has broken the pattern. Report the result as an estimate under this method.

Worked example 2 · age, and the rounding trap

A tree's diameter was measured at three equal five-year intervals: 30 cm in 1970, 33 cm in 1975 and 36 cm in 1980. Estimate its age in 1980 by the periodic-measurement method.

P₁(33 − 30) ÷ (30 × 5) = 3/150 = 0.02
P₂(36 − 33) ÷ (33 × 5) = 3/165 = 1/55 = 0.0181818…  not 0.018
log P₁ − log P₂−1.698970 − (−1.740363) = 0.041393
log d₂ − log d₁1.518514 − 1.477121 = 0.041393
S0.041393 ÷ 0.041393 = 1.000000 exactly
Age at 19701 ÷ (0.02 × 1) = 50 years
Age at 198050 + 10 = 60 years
About 60 years in 1980, under this method — computed at full precision

Why S came out exactly 1. P₁/P₂ = 0.02 × 55 = 1.1, and d₂/d₁ = 33 ÷ 30 = 1.1. The ratio of two identical logarithms is 1 in any base. The example was built that way. Note that 0.018182 is only a display: 0.02 ÷ 0.018182 is not exactly 1.1, so keep 3/165 in the calculator.

The rounding trap. Round P₂ to 0.018 and S becomes 1.105449, giving 45.2305 years at 1970 and 55.2305 at 1980. That is 4.77 years, or 7.95 % of 60, from a 1 % cut in P₂: a change of only 0.0001818. Whole-year answers, 60 and 55, differ by 8.33 %. For this module the answer is 60 under this method; 55 is taught only to demonstrate the trap.

A general property worth noticing

Whenever the two periodic increments are equal in absolute terms, P₁/P₂ = d₂/d₁ identically, so S = 1 for any such data set and the age reduces to d₁ divided by the annual diameter increment: 30 ÷ 0.6 = 50 here. On such data the method is simply a constant-rate extrapolation back to zero diameter.

Method 5 · counting the rings of a felled tree

The best available evidence where rings are annual, distinct and cross-dated — and unavailable on a standing tree you are not felling. A distinct ring is not automatically an annual one.

Rings at a stump give the age at that height

Not from germination. The years the tree spent growing up to that height must be added back: perhaps 3–8 years to reach breast height on a good site, and as much as 15 years for a stem suppressed under shade. These are illustrative local figures, not constants, and a wrong local figure biases every age.

The increment borer

Extracts a core from bark to pith on a standing tree. The rings on the core are counted, giving the age at the height bored — corrected as above.

When rings lie

False rings — a drought or defoliation mid-season produces a band that looks annual. Missing rings — a severely suppressed tree may add no measurable wood in a bad year. Indistinct rings — where trees can grow year-round, many form no clear annual rings, and some broadleaves in warmer, wetter forests may be hard to read. This is species- and site-specific, so validate rings before counting. Where they fail, permanent plots and Method 4 are alternatives, with their own assumptions.

A core is a radius

Let w be the summed radial width of the outer n complete annual increments. Then 2w estimates the diameter growth of those n years, if the stem is round and the radius is representative. An eccentric stem needs representative radii. Count increments, the spaces between ring boundaries, not boundaries. The doubling catches people out repeatedly.

Section 3Growth and increment

Increment is the increase in girth, diameter, basal area, height,
volume, quality, price or value of trees or crops during a given period
Stop on the last three words in that list

Increment is not only physical. A stand whose timber has become more valuable per cubic metre has put on increment in value without adding a single cubic metre of wood. That idea underpins the whole of forest economics.

The shape of a lifetime. Typically the rate is slow at first, becomes rapid at a certain age reaching an optimum, then slows again. Cambial growth usually continues while the tree lives, though height growth slows greatly.

Negative increment. With increasing age the forces of decay become more active than the increment. A hollowing, rotting veteran may lose more wood to decay each year than its cambium adds, so its net volume falls: gross growth continues while net increment is negative. Whether an old tree stays or goes depends on the objective, and habitat may keep it.

Growth in what? The age at which the highest rate is reached is different for different parameters — diameter, height, form, volume. A common order is height, diameter, basal area, then volume, because basal area goes with d²; that order is illustrative, not a law for every tree. A single “growth rate” for a tree is meaningless without saying growth in what.

Why breast height stays breast height

A stem does not elongate below the tip. Drive a nail into a stem at breast height and fifty years later it is still at the same height — but buried, because the stem around it has thickened. That is what makes the breast-height mark a stable, repeatable point to re-measure decade after decade, provided every crew uses the same reference height, fixed by the governing protocol: 1.37 m in Bhutan's Second NFI, 1.30 m in many international manuals. The examples in this unit use 1.3 m as a teaching convention.

Section 4The four classes of increment

Increment — in diameter, height or volume alike — is classified only by the period to which it relates. Same tree, same growth, four different periods of accounting.

ClassPeriodWhat you must be able to say
1c.a.i current annual incrementA single year V(A) − V(A−1), a one-year difference; dV/dA is its continuous-model analogue. Rarely recoverable from annual tape readings, so p.a.i is used. It can be estimated from dendrometers, tree rings or a growth model
2p.a.i periodic annual incrementAny short period An average: [V(A₂) − V(A₁)] ÷ (A₂ − A₁). It belongs to the interval, not to one age
3T.I total incrementOrigin to the age of cutting The sum of the c.a.i. With no removals or losses it equals the standing volume; otherwise live stock is less than total production
4m.a.i mean annual incrementOrigin to a given age V(A) ÷ A, under the stated yield definition. For part of the age it is the periodic m.a.i; at the rotation age it is the final m.a.i

Why c.a.i is replaced by p.a.i

Say it precisely

c.a.i is not unmeasurable in principle. It is too small and too variable to recover from ordinary annual tape measurements. A blue pine adds about 4 mm of diameter in a good year; two people reading a girth tape on the same stem may differ by 2–3 mm of diameter. The signal is of the same order as the noise: a weak signal, not always an invisible one. Growth is also a difference of two readings, so it carries both errors, about √2 times one reading's error if they are independent.

Widen the window to ten years and the same tree adds 40 mm, while the reader discrepancy is still 2–3 mm — now more than ten to one. That is the entire argument for periodic annual increment, and it generalises: when the signal is small, lengthen the period.

p.a.i = (size at the end − size at the beginning) ÷ number of years in the period

And the price you pay. p.a.i is an average: it tells you nothing about what happened within the period. A stand released by a thinning in year three gives the same p.a.i as one that grew steadily. Five to ten years is the usual compromise for permanent sample plots.

Worked example 3 · p.a.i and m.a.i from a permanent plot

A blue pine plot, planted in 1985, measured in 2015 at 148 m³/ha and in 2025 at 226 m³/ha. Assume one volume definition, and no removals, deaths or recruits.

Formulaep.a.i = (V₂ − V₁) ÷ (A₂ − A₁) · m.a.i = V ÷ A
Ages2015 − 1985 = 30 yr · 2025 − 1985 = 40 yr
p.a.i(226 − 148) ÷ (40 − 30) = 7.8 m³/ha/yr
m.a.i, 2015148 ÷ 30 = 4.93 m³/ha/yr
m.a.i, 2025226 ÷ 40 = 5.65 m³/ha/yr
p.a.i 7.8; m.a.i rose from 4.93 to 5.65 across the interval

Note that m.a.i needs the age, which the two measurements alone do not give. m.a.i is measured from origin, not from the last measurement.

Stock is not total production

The simple examples assume one volume definition and no thinning, removal, mortality or recruitment. A real plot does not oblige. Total yield is live stock plus all earlier removals. Net increment is growth after natural losses. Gross increment is growth before them, including trees felled or dying in the period.

Worked example 3a · net and gross increment

Stock rises from 200 to 230 m³/ha over 10 years. Removals were 20 and mortality loss 10 m³/ha.

Stock change230 − 200 = 30, or only 3 per year
Net(230 − 200 + 20) ÷ 10 = 5 m³/ha/yr
Gross(230 − 200 + 20 + 10) ÷ 10 = 6 m³/ha/yr
Checkgross − mortality per year = 6 − 1 = 5

Ingrowth: count it once. Trees crossing the minimum size are already inside the end stock here. Report them separately only if you take them out of the stock change. Inventories differ, so state the convention.

Section 5Culmination and rotation

For smooth, single-peaked growth, c.a.i falls through m.a.i from above at the culmination of the m.a.i. Plot p.a.i at the interval midpoint or as a horizontal segment, not as an annual value at one age. Separately, in annual data with V(0) = 0, first-year c.a.i equals first-year m.a.i. That is an identity of the arithmetic, not a second crossing on a smooth curve. “Exactly twice” is not universal: disturbance, management and repeated growth flushes can give other patterns.

Why they cross at the culmination

Annual data. A new value above a running average lifts it, below lowers it, equal leaves it still. So m.a.i rises while c.a.i exceeds last year's m.a.i and falls once it is below, and the peak is that switch. Exact equality need not occur: scores of 40, 60 and 20 give averages of 40, 50 and 40, a peak with no later score equal to the average.

Smooth V(A). The slope of m.a.i is (dV/dA − V/A) ÷ A. Equality, dV/dA = V/A, marks a stationary point; c.a.i − m.a.i changing from positive to negative is what makes it a maximum.

Culmination of the m.a.i identifies the biological rotation
for maximum mean annual volume production it identifies one rotation — it is not, by itself, the rotation that will be adopted
What p.a.i > m.a.i does and does not tell you

With p.a.i above the end-of-period m.a.i, the m.a.i rose across that interval, assuming consistent age and yield definitions. It does not prove the m.a.i is rising today, or that no earlier peak passed. It does not by itself determine whether a stand should be harvested.

What actually fixes a rotation

  • It maximises volume, not value. A later cut can pay if trees move into a higher-value product class, even past the m.a.i peak.
  • It ignores the cost of waiting. A financial rotation discounts revenues and costs, as in net present value or land expectation value. The land-expectation rotation can be shorter or longer than the biological rotation.
  • It assumes volume is the object. For protection forest, conservation or habitat the rotation may be far longer — or may not exist at all.
In a Bhutanese management plan

The age finally adopted answers to biodiversity and habitat objectives, watershed protection, livelihood and rural timber demand, regeneration, carbon, cultural values, financial return, and the legal prescription of the approved plan — with the culmination as the biological benchmark against which those choices are judged.

The peak is flat — and that is a gift

In the deck's yield series, 11.00 at age 35, 11.13 at 40, 11.00 at 45. Five years either side of the culmination costs about 1.1 % of production. Further out it costs more: age 50 gives 3.8 % less and age 30 gives 5.6 % less. Near its peak the optimum is a plateau, not a knife edge, within those limits.

Section 6Increment percentage

Growth as a proportion of the stock already there, per year. The arithmetic is that of an interest rate, but it measures wood, not money. It is not a financial return: that needs timber value, prices, quality, costs, risk and the timing of cash flows.

SymbolMeaning
S0size of the parameter at the beginning of the growth period; S0 > 0
Snsize at the end of the period
nyears in the period

The parameter may be diameter, basal area, height or volume. The formulae do not care — they are not volume formulae — provided both sizes share one unit and one basis.

1 · Simple interest   P = (Sn − S0) ÷ (n S0) × 100
2 · Compound interest   P = [(Sn/S0)1/n − 1] × 100
3 · Pressler's   Pp = (Sn − S0) ÷ (Sn + S0) × 200/n

Recovering the past diameter by boring

d0,ub = dn,ub − 2w w = sum of the radial widths of the outer n complete annual increments
dn,ub = dn,ob − 2tn do this first: tn is today's one-sided bark thickness
Keep one bark basis

Rings lie inside the bark, so w is under-bark growth. dn,ob − 2w is not a valid past diameter: it inflates the base and understates a percentage. An over-bark present against a true under-bark past overstates growth instead. There is no single direction of error. The rule is consistency.

Worked example 4 · recovering the past diameter
Present diameter under bark65 cm
w, across 10 increments9.5 cm  (a radius)
Diameter growth in 10 yr2w = 2 × 9.5 = 19 cm
Diameter 10 years ago65 − 19 = 46 cm

9.5 cm over 10 increments is 9.5 mm of radial growth a year — rapid, a well-grown tree on a good site, not a typical rate.

Worked example 5 · one tree, three formulae

S0 = 46 cm, Sn = 65 cm, n = 10 years, both under bark.

Simple(65 − 46) ÷ (10 × 46) × 100 = 19/460 × 100 = 4.13 %
Compound[(65/46)1/10 − 1] × 100 = (1.0351792 − 1) × 100 = 3.52 %
Pressler19/111 × 200/10 = 19/111 × 20 = 3.42 %
Simple 4.13 > compound 3.52 > Pressler 3.42 % a year

The rule, with its conditions. For S0 > 0, positive growth and n ≥ 1: simple ≥ compound > Pressler, with simple = compound at n = 1. So strict simple > compound needs n > 1. Do not extend this unqualified to periods under a year or to negative growth.

Why simple reads highest here. It divides by the original 46 for all ten years. Compound divides by a base that grows each year, and Pressler divides by the mean of the two ends. Over ten years simple read 14–17 % above compound in the two examples in the deck, and the gap keeps widening with n. Pressler's closeness to compound depends on the size ratio: for a fourfold increase in ten years, compound is 14.87 % a year and Pressler 12.00 %.

Which to use

Simple — short periods and rough work. Compound — the constant annual physical rate that carries S0 to Sn; exact only if the rate really was constant. Pressler — a quick estimate without roots, when the size ratio is modest. None of the three is a financial comparison on its own.

Units cancel once matched: with S0 and Sn in one unit every term is a ratio, so the answer is the same in cubic feet or cubic metres.

Volume percentage often exceeds diameter percentage for the same tree: about double if height and form stayed fixed, because volume goes with d²h. It depends on them, so name the parameter as well as the formula.

Section 7Error and quality control

  • Random — reading the tape, judging a ring boundary, small differences between observers. Shrinks with more independent measurements, though less if they are correlated, as cores from one tree are, and a small sample may not cancel.
  • Systematic — a stretched tape, a crew measuring at a slightly different height at each re-measurement, consistently counting a false ring, a biased height-age correction, mixed bark bases. More measurements improve precision; they do not repair a biased method.
  • Not error at all — genuine variation between trees and between years is biology. Sample it; do not average it away.

For increment work specifically:

  • Re-measure at exactly the same point. Mark it; on a permanent plot record the height.
  • Use the same instrument, or calibrate the new one against the old.
  • Carry full precision through the calculation and round once, at the end — Worked example 2 shows an error of nearly five years from rounding in the third decimal place.
  • Record the date of each re-measurement, not just the year.

Section 8In Bhutanese forestry

Increment is the quantity a management plan is built on. Everything in this unit ends in a decision that somebody has to sign.

  • Allowable harvest. An FMU or community-forest plan sets the annual cut against the increment the forest actually puts on. Harvest held above net increment draws the growing stock down.
  • Growth monitoring. Permanent sample plots re-measured on a five- to ten-year cycle give the p.a.i that tells a Division whether a stand is responding to management.
  • Rotation decisions. Culmination of the m.a.i is the biological benchmark; the rotation adopted also answers to biodiversity, watershed, livelihood, carbon, cultural and legal objectives.
  • Climate resilience. Repeated increment measurement is how a drought year, an insect outbreak or a shift in growing season is detected at all — a declining p.a.i is often the first evidence.
Community forestry

A community-forest management plan must state a sustainable annual harvest of timber, fuelwood and poles. Members' own observation of which stands are growing well is combined with plot re-measurement — local ecological knowledge and formal measurement together, not one instead of the other.

Section 9Practice questions

  1. From a yield series, tabulate the m.a.i and p.a.i, plot both, locate the culmination and state the rotation of maximum volume production. Show all working.
  2. Describe the four traditional methods of estimating a standing tree's age, add increment coring, and evaluate each.
  3. A tree measured in 2000, 2005 and 2010 had diameters 25, 28 and 31 cm. Estimate its age in 2010, explain the value of S, and state the assumptions.
  4. Explain, with a labelled sketch, the relationship between c.a.i and m.a.i, including the first-year identity in annual data and why it differs from the crossing.
  5. A core gives w = 7.2 cm across the outer 10 annual increments; present diameter under bark is 58 cm. Compute the growth percentage by all three formulae and comment on the ordering.
  6. A manager's stand has culminated in m.a.i but timber prices are expected to rise sharply in five years. Should the stand be cut now? Justify.
  7. Show that for a smooth V(A) m.a.i is stationary where c.a.i = m.a.i, state what makes it a maximum, and explain how annual data differ.
  8. Distinguish random from systematic error in age and growth estimation, giving two field examples of each and stating how you would control them.
  9. Over a period a stand's p.a.i is 12 and its end-of-period m.a.i is 9 m³/ha/yr. What happened to the m.a.i, and what does this not tell you?
  10. Why can m.a.i never be negative while c.a.i can?

Section 10Summary

  • Four traditional standing-tree methods (records, appearance, whorls, periodic measurements), then coring and a stump count. Rings at a stump give age at that height — add the years to reach it.
  • P₁ = (d₂−d₁)/(d₁T); P₂ = (d₃−d₂)/(d₂T); S = (logP₁−logP₂)/(logd₂−logd₁); Age = 1/(P₁S): a model-based estimate.
  • Four classes of increment, distinguished only by the period: c.a.i, p.a.i, T.I, m.a.i.
  • c.a.i is replaced by p.a.i because one year's growth is hard to resolve with an ordinary tape — not because it is unmeasurable in principle.
  • Live stock is not total production: state removals, mortality and ingrowth.
  • c.a.i falls through m.a.i at the culmination of the m.a.i, which identifies the biological rotation and nothing more. First-year equality in annual data is arithmetic, not a crossing.
  • Three growth percentages: for positive growth and n ≥ 1, simple ≥ compound > Pressler. All three are physical rates, not financial returns.
  • A core is a radius: d0,ub = dn,ub − 2w, on one bark basis.
  • Age converts a measurement into a rate.
