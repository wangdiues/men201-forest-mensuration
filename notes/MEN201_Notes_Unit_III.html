Section 1Learning outcomes

  1. Define crown, crown base, crown length, crown-base height, crown midpoint height, crown diameter, bole height and canopy.
  2. Distinguish crown closure, canopy cover and canopy closure, and say which one a given protocol wants.
  3. Compute crown length, live-crown ratio, crown midpoint height and crown projection area.
  4. Carry out Bhutan's official canopy cover assessment — the 500 m² plot, the 2.5 m grid, the GRS crown densitometer and the calculation.
  5. Classify a plot as forest or non-forest and as high forest or not, and state what follows in law.
  6. Compare the official figure with ocular and spherical-densiometer estimates and explain why they differ.
  7. Identify the main sources of error and the good practice that removes them.

Section 2Crown terminology

Crown
The upper branchy part of the tree above the bole.
Crown base
The lowest live branch — the level at which the crown is taken to begin. An isolated epicormic sprout is not the crown base; use the lowest branch of the continuous live crown, and record the rule you applied.
Crown length (CL)
Crown base to the top of the tree. Obtained by subtraction: CL = H − BH.
Crown-base height
Ground to the base of the live crown. Unit IV uses the same term for the same quantity.
Crown midpoint height
Ground to the midpoint of the live crown: BH + CL/2.
Live-crown ratio (LCR)
CL / H, usually as a percentage. The standard field indicator of tree vigour.
Bole height (BH)
Ground to the base of the crown — the clean, branch-free length of stem.
Crown diameter
The horizontal width of the crown, usually the mean of two or four radii measured on cardinal bearings with a vertical sighting device.
Canopy
The collective crown cover of all the trees in a stand, considered as one layer.
This module never says “crown height”

The bare term carries three incompatible meanings in the literature: the height of the crown midpoint, the vertical extent of the crown (= crown length), and the height of the crown base (= crown-base height). None of the three books is wrong; they use different conventions.

Use one of the four named terms instead — crown-base height, crown length, crown midpoint height, live-crown ratio — and nobody has to guess. Where a book says “crown height”, your first job is to find out which of the three it means.

Section 3Measuring crown dimensions

CL = H − BH  ·  crown midpoint height = BH + CL/2  ·  LCR = CL / H

Crown projection area

The crown projection area (CPA) is the area of the vertical projection of the crown onto the ground — not a real object, but the shape a plumb line traced round the crown edge would enclose. Taking the crown as circular with mean diameter CD:

CPA = π (CD/2)²
Worked example 1 · the dimensions of one crown

A blue pine (Pinus wallichiana): total height 24.6 m, bole height 9.8 m, crown diameters 8.2 m and 7.6 m on perpendicular axes.

Crown length24.6 − 9.8 = 14.8 m
Live-crown ratio14.8 / 24.6 × 100 = 60.2 %
Crown midpoint height9.8 + 14.8/2 = 9.8 + 7.4 = 17.2 m
Mean crown diameter(8.2 + 7.6)/2 = 7.9 m
Crown projection areaπ × 3.95² = 49.0 m²
14.8 m · 60.2 % · 17.2 m · 49.0 m²

The two wrong answers to watch for on the midpoint: 7.4 m, from forgetting to add the bole height back, and 14.8 m, from confusing crown midpoint height with crown length.

Errors add — they do not cancel

Crown length is a difference of two measurements. If a single clinometer reading carries a standard error of about half a metre, the difference carries about 0.7 m — errors add in quadrature. So 14.8 m is really 14.8 ± 0.7 m, and quoting it as 14.83 m is dishonest.

Live-crown ratio and vigour

LCR is the one derived crown variable foresters use every day. A ratio below about one third signals a suppressed tree that has been overtopped and is losing the capacity to respond to release. Divide crown length by total height, never by bole height.

Section 4Three quantities, not one

This is the conceptual heart of the unit, and the distinction has legal force in Bhutan.

QuantityWhat it measuresNatureCan exceed 100 %?
Crown cover Σ of individual crown projection areas ÷ ground area, without correcting for overlap arealYes — and when it does, that is information, not an error: the crowns overlap
Canopy cover The proportion of ground covered by the vertical projection of tree crowns, overlap counted once arealNo
Canopy closure The proportion of the sky hemisphere obscured by vegetation when viewed from a single point angularNo
Canopy cover is not “what is shaded at noon”

Real shade moves with the solar angle. The definition is strictly vertical projection, which is exactly why aerial photographs, satellite imagery and lidar measure it naturally — and why it is the quantity that ends up in national forest cover statistics.

Closure is angular; cover is areal

A densiometer with a wide field of view sees crowns at an angle and therefore reads higher than a strictly vertical measurement. That is not a fault in the instrument — it is measuring a different quantity. One stand, three observers, three legitimate answers.

Crown cover, overlap and canopy cover

canopy cover = crown cover − overlap

The only honest way to obtain the overlap is to map the crowns — on a plan sheet, on an aerial photograph, or in GIS. If crowns were placed at random, the probability that a point is covered by no crown falls off exponentially with crown cover; that is a model, not a measurement, and it assumes crowns are small relative to the plot and independently placed.

Section 5Bhutan's official canopy procedure

The governing method — FMID 2024

Forest Canopy Cover Assessment Guidelines 2024, Forest Monitoring and Information Division, Department of Forests and Park Services. This is what the Department actually does on State Reserved Forest Land, and it is the procedure a graduate will be handed in their first posting. Everything in Section 6 below is comparative.

Definitions and thresholds

Forest — three criteria, all of which must be met:

  • land spanning more than 0.5 ha;
  • trees exceeding 5 m in height;
  • canopy cover of more than 10 %.

High forest — trees grown by natural seedling or by plantation where the forest canopy cover is 40 % and more, if the trees are of economic value and social benefit (FNCA 2023).

The sentence that makes the procedure matter

FNCA 2023, s. 46 (2): “Forestry clearance shall not be issued for any activity in State Reserved Forest Land which fall in: (a) high forest.” A crew reading 38 % or 42 % is settling whether a road, a school or a transmission line may lawfully proceed there.

Step 1 · direct measurement, or sampling?

Decided by area alone, not by judgement.

Area of interestObservation pointsApproach
up to 500 m²100direct measurement
> 500 – 2 500 m²150direct measurement
> 2 500 – 5 000 m²200direct measurement
> 5 000 m² (> 0.5 ha)100 per plotestablish sample plots

Where the 2.5 m spacing comes from:

grid area = area ÷ points = 500 ÷ 100 = 5 m²  →  spacing = √5 = 2.23 m → 2.5 m

Spacing must be at least 1 m and no more than 2.5 m. If the arithmetic gives more than 2.5 m, do not stretch it — establish sample plots instead.

Above 5 000 m², the plot count comes from a 100 m × 100 m grid: area ÷ 10 000. A 12 ha school site gives 120 000 ÷ 10 000 = 12 plots. The final count depends on the regular points GIS generates for the shape of the parcel, so it may be a little more or less. Above 30 plots the grid opens to 200 m × 200 m.

Step 2 · the 500 m² circular plot

Radius12.62 m  (π × 12.62² = 500 m²)
Readings100 minimum — so each reading is exactly 1 %
Grid2.5 m × 2.5 m
Points89 systematic + 11 additional

A 2.5 m grid across a 12.62 m radius circle yields about 89 points inside the circle; eleven more are added to reach the hundred. The eleven must be taken from the same quadrant in every plot.

Why the quadrant rule exists

If the crew chooses where the extra points go, they will choose convenient ground — which in a forest means open ground, away from thickets. Fixing the quadrant in advance removes the choice, and with it the bias. Same reason the systematic grid is laid out in GIS rather than paced: a good protocol removes discretion exactly where discretion would introduce bias.

This is the same plot geometry the National Forest Inventory and Forest Management Unit inventories use — one plot, one radius, across all Bhutanese forest measurement, which is also why the expansion factor in Unit VI is 20.

Step 3 · the GRS crown densitometer

  1. Hold the instrument at the observation point and level it — align the sighting level and the bubble levels.
  2. Sight vertically upward and look at the inner black circle.
  3. Record 1 if more than 50 % of the inner black circle is covered by canopy.
  4. Record 0 otherwise.
  5. Repeat until every observation is complete.
  6. Total the 1s on the tally sheet, in the field — one sheet per plot.
It is not the spherical densiometer

A sighting tube with cross-hairs and a bubble, not a convex mirror with an engraved grid. One point, one digit. No dot count, no mean of four bearings, no × 1.04. Students who have met the spherical densiometer elsewhere will try to import its arithmetic; it does not apply.

The calculation

Canopy cover % = (Σ 1s ÷ 100) × 100 one plot · with 100 independent readings, every reading is one percentage point
Canopy cover % = Σ 1s ÷ (100 × number of plots) × 100 several plots
Worked example 2 · three plots
1s recorded71 + 64 + 78 = 213
Divisor100 × 3 = 300
Canopy cover213 ÷ 300 × 100 = 71 %
71 % — high forest
The exception

Where more than 100 points are read — a 150-point direct measurement, or a rectangular plot with more transect points — the divisor is the total number of observation points, not 100.

Rectangular plots

For linear works only — farm and forest roads, low-tension lines, irrigation channels — where a circular plot cannot be laid. At least 3 transects spaced 1–2.5 m, 100 points minimum, first transect on an edge and ideally one central. Beyond 500 m² of corridor, a further rectangular plot every 500 m along the alignment. High-tension corridors are wide enough for circular plots — use them.

Boundary rule: if the plot centre lies inside the area of interest, read the whole plot, including points that fall outside the boundary.

Section 6Comparative methods

These are legitimate methods and much published research uses them. None of them is the prescribed method for a Bhutanese canopy cover assessment.

FamilyHowTrade-off
Ocular estimationLook up, judge the percentage, assign a classFree; subjective
Spherical densiometerCount imagined dots on a convex mirror, convertCheap and fast; wide angle of view, so it measures nearer closure
Hemispherical photographyFisheye image of the sky, thresholded and analysedRepeatable and archivable; equipment and processing
Transects, grids, tubesLine intercept, point intercept, moosehorn, canopy scopeObjective — every decision is yes or no; slow
Remote sensingAerial photographs, satellite imagery, airborne lidarWhole landscape; costly, and needs field calibration

The trade-off that runs through the section: cheap and fast methods are subjective; objective and repeatable methods are slow or expensive. No method is all four — which is exactly why a country adopts one and requires everyone to use it.

Ocular estimation — the documented biases

Do not be dismissive: it is the method most used in the world, and a trained eye is genuinely good. An untrained eye is genuinely bad, and the difference is calibration. The biases are well documented:

  • observers over-estimate below about 30 % — scattered crowns look more numerous than they are;
  • observers under-estimate above about 70 % — small gaps are conspicuous;
  • observers pull towards round numbers, so a set of ocular estimates has spikes at multiples of ten.

The spherical densiometer

A convex mirror with a grid of 24 squares engraved on it, plus a spirit level. The 24 squares are not the counting unit — the observer imagines four equally spaced dots in each square, giving 96 dots, and counts those.

% open sky = (dots counted) × 1.04  →  closure % = 100 − % open

The factor 1.04 converts 96 dots to a percentage (100/96 = 1.0417). Counting covered dots directly is also in use; both are correct applied consistently, but mixing them gives 20 % inside a closed forest.

Worked example 3 · reducing a densiometer point

Four bearings at one point give open-sky dot counts of 5, 4, 6 and 4.

Mean dots(5+4+6+4)/4 = 4.75
% open sky4.75 × 1.04 = 4.94 %
Canopy closure100 − 4.94 = 95.1 %
95.1 % closure — note this is closure, not cover

Section 7Sources of error

Random — reduced by more readings

  • Judging borderline points at the 50 % threshold.
  • Small differences in how level the instrument is held.
  • Natural variation between points within the plot.

Systematic — not reduced by more readings

  • The observer's head reflected inside the grid and counted as canopy — reads high.
  • Instrument not level — on a slope, consistently in one direction.
  • A wide angle of view compared against vertical cover — reads high.
  • Leaf-off season in a deciduous stand — reads low.
  • Understorey included when the protocol wanted trees only — reads high.
  • Sampling only where walking is easy — along the road, away from the thickets. Usually reads low, because thickets are dense.
Which one to eliminate first

Most students say the instrument. The right answer is usually the sampling — where the points were placed — because instrument error is small and bounded, and sampling bias is unbounded. No amount of care with the instrument corrects a badly placed set of points.

Ten mistakes that cost marks and credibility

  1. Using crown closure, canopy cover and canopy closure as synonyms.
  2. Multiplying densiometer dots by 1.04 and forgetting to subtract from 100.
  3. Taking a single densiometer reading at a point instead of four bearings.
  4. Letting the observer's head reflection fall inside the grid.
  5. Dividing crown length by bole height instead of total height for LCR.
  6. Writing the bare term “crown height” instead of naming one of the four agreed terms.
  7. Counting the 24 squares instead of the 96 imagined dots, then applying 1.04 anyway.
  8. Insisting that a crown cover of 120 % must be an error — it is legitimate, and simply uncorrected for overlap.
  9. Comparing a densiometer figure with a lidar figure and concluding one of them is wrong.
  10. Reporting a canopy percentage with no method, no angle of view and no layers stated.

Number 10 persists longest into a career, because nobody marks it. A canopy figure without its method attached cannot be checked, compared or repeated, and is therefore not a measurement.

Section 8In Bhutanese forestry

Of everything in MEN 201, the number produced in this unit is the one that most directly becomes a legal decision.

  • Forest classification. Canopy cover classifies State Reserved Forest Land as forest or non-forest, and at 40 % and above as high forest — where forestry clearance shall not be issued.
  • National reporting. The Constitution requires a minimum of 60 % forest cover for all time. It is monitored by this quantity, which is why the method was standardised in 2024.
  • Habitat condition. Canopy governs light at the forest floor, and with it regeneration, understorey composition, ground temperature and moisture — so it governs what can live there.
  • Watershed and climate. Interception and throughfall depend on canopy, and canopy loss is among the first measurable signatures of drought stress, pest outbreak and windthrow.
Community forestry

A community-forest management plan must describe stand condition, and canopy cover is the cheapest and most repeatable descriptor a group can collect for itself. Members can be trained on the GRS densitometer in an afternoon, and the record is theirs.

Why one national method mattered

Before 2024 the same stand returned different figures in different Divisions, because offices used different instruments and different definitions. A standard method is not bureaucracy — it is what makes a measurement mean the same thing in Haa and in Trashigang.

Section 9Field practical

Use the printed tally sheet.

Part A · one tree. Measure DBH at the prescribed reference height from the uphill side; total height and bole height with a clinometer from a measured horizontal distance; record the crown base independently within your group before comparing — the spread between groups is the lesson; four crown radii on the cardinal bearings; then compute crown length, live-crown ratio, crown midpoint height and crown projection area.

Part B · the official procedure. Establish the 12.62 m plot from the supplied centre; lay the pre-generated 2.5 m grid; read 89 + 11 points with the GRS densitometer, the 11 from your assigned quadrant; score 1 or 0; total in the field; compute cover for one plot and pooled across groups.

Part C · comparative, then reconcile. An independent ocular estimate at plot centre and five spherical-densiometer points. Rank the three figures: the official cover figure should be the lowest and the spherical-densiometer figure the highest. If your order differs, explain it rather than correcting it. State one figure as a clearance file would carry it — value, method, guideline reference, date, observer — and classify the plot.

Section 10Practice questions

  1. A tree has total height 18.4 m and bole height 6.9 m; crown diameters are 7.8 m and 6.6 m. Find the crown length, the live-crown ratio, the crown midpoint height, the mean crown diameter and the crown projection area.
  2. Define crown cover, canopy cover and canopy closure. Which can exceed 100 %, and why is that not an error?
  3. Why is canopy cover, and not canopy closure, the quantity that appears in national forest cover statistics?
  4. Derive the 2.5 m point spacing for a 500 m² plot with 100 observation points.
  5. A plot returns 43 ones out of 100 points. Classify it as forest / non-forest and high forest / not, and state what follows under FNCA 2023.
  6. Four plots return 58, 61, 55 and 66 ones. Compute the canopy cover for the area.
  7. A 150-point direct measurement returns 96 ones. Compute the canopy cover. What is the commonest mistake here?
  8. Explain why the eleven random points must come from the same quadrant in every plot.
  9. A densiometer point gives open-sky dot counts of 8, 6, 9 and 7 on four bearings. Compute the canopy closure. Why is this not directly comparable with the figure in question 5?
  10. Three observers assess the same stand and report 62 %, 71 % and 88 %. None has made an arithmetic error. Explain how this is possible.

Worked solutions to questions 1, 4, 6, 7 and 9 are in the calculation handbook.

Section 11Summary

  • Four crown terms, no ambiguity: crown-base height, crown length, crown midpoint height, live-crown ratio. Never the bare term “crown height”.
  • CL = H − BH · LCR = CL/H · crown midpoint = BH + CL/2 · CPA = π(CD/2)²
  • Crown cover can exceed 100 %; canopy cover is areal and vertical; canopy closure is angular. Closure reads higher than cover.
  • Bhutan's official procedure: 500 m² circular plot, 12.62 m radius, 2.5 m grid, 89 + 11 points, GRS crown densitometer, binary 1/0, Σ1s ÷ total points × 100.
  • Thresholds: forest above 10 %; high forest at 40 % and above, where forestry clearance shall not be issued.
  • Sampling bias is unbounded; instrument error is not. Fix where the points go first.
  • A canopy figure without its method is not a measurement.
