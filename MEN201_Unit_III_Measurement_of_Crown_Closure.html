The decisions that depend on a canopy figure

Why crown closure is worth measuring properly

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit III · Session 5
What counts as forest

The international definition of forest is a canopy cover threshold — more than 10 % cover, over more than 0.5 ha, with trees able to reach 5 m.

Silvicultural prescription

Thinning, shelterwood and gap felling are written as canopy targets, because light at the forest floor is very nearly the complement of closure.

Regeneration

Chirpine needs an open canopy to regenerate; many broadleaves need shade. The wrong closure gives the wrong species mix.

Hydrology and soil

Canopy intercepts rainfall and absorbs the energy of the rest. Closure is a first-order variable in erosion and landslide risk.

Habitat description

Many species are defined by the canopy condition they require. Habitat maps are, in large part, canopy maps.

Stocking and growth

Closure indexes how fully a site is occupied. It is a quick, cheap proxy for stand density where basal area cannot be measured.

69.71 %

Forest cover in Bhutan, as reported in the second National Forest Inventory. That figure exists because somebody drew a canopy cover threshold and then measured against it — with the methods in this session.

Section A · Orientation

The crown is the tree's working machinery

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit III · Session 5

The crown is the upper branchy part of the tree above the bole — the assembly of branches, twigs and foliage in which all photosynthesis takes place.

  • Everything the stem becomes was manufactured in the crown; crown size therefore predicts growth better than stem size does
  • Crown dimensions are used in thinning, in vigour assessment, in biomass equations and in habitat description
  • Crown measurements are also the cheapest stand variables to obtain — several can be read from an aerial photograph
  • A crown belongs to one tree; a canopy belongs to a stand. Keep the two words apart from the start
The distinction that governs the whole session

One tree has a crown. A stand has a canopy, made of many crowns. Crown is a tree-level word; canopy is a stand-level word.

Tree level · stand level

Section A · Definitions, part 1 — the vertical dimensions

Terms related to the crown · measured upwards

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit III · Session 5
Crown

The upper branchy part of the tree above the bole.

Crown base

The lowest live branch of the tree — the level at which the crown is taken to begin.

Crown length

The distance from the crown base to the top of the tree. Obtained by subtraction: total height − bole height.

Crown height

The height from the ground to the midpoint of the live crown. Conventions differ — see slide 10.

Total height

The distance from the tip of the leading shoot to ground level.

Bole height

The distance from the ground to the base of the crown — the clean, branch-free length of stem.

Field warning · crown base

An isolated epicormic sprout is not the crown base. Use the lowest branch of the continuous live crown, and record the rule you applied.

Arithmetic that must always hold

Total height = bole height + crown length. If your three recorded numbers do not satisfy this, one of them is wrong.

Section A · Definitions, part 2 — spread, utility and the stand

Crown diameter, commercial bole height and canopy

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit III · Session 5
Crown diameter

The horizontal diameter, or spread, of the crown — the width of the crown's shadow if the sun were directly overhead.

Commercial bole height

The height of the bole that is fit for utilisation as timber. It ends at a fork, a serious defect, or a minimum utilisable diameter — so it is an economic limit, not a botanical one.

Canopy

The uppermost layer of vegetation formed by the crowns of trees. It represents the horizontal extent and the vertical structure of foliage that influences light penetration, microclimate and forest dynamics.

Horizontal extent

How much of the ground is covered, seen from above. This is what Section B calls canopy cover.

Vertical structure

How the foliage is arranged in layers — emergent, main canopy, understorey. Not captured by a single cover figure.

Consequences

Light penetration, microclimate and forest dynamics — the reasons a canopy figure is worth collecting at all.

Read the definition again

Sections B and C of this session are an extended attempt to put a defensible number on just the first half of that sentence — the horizontal extent.

Section A · Field procedure

Measuring the vertical crown dimensions

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit III · Session 5

No vertical crown dimension is measured directly. Every one is a difference of two hypsometer readings.

Procedure
1
Choose a station from which both the tip and the crown base are visible. Do not accept a station that shows only one.
2
Measure the horizontal distance to the stem — on a slope this is not the slope distance.
3
Read the angle to the tip of the leading shoot, and to the base of the tree. Their difference gives total height H.
4
Read the angle to the lowest live branch. This gives bole height BH.
5
Subtract: CL = H − BH, then CH = BH + CL/2. Nothing else is recorded.
Errors add — they do not cancel

If one clinometer reading carries a standard error of about 0.5 m, a difference of two carries about 0.7 m. A crown length of 14.8 m is 14.8 ± 0.7 m. Do not write 14.83 m.

One station · three angles · two subtractions
Three field traps
  • On a lean, the tip is not above the base — stand perpendicular to the direction of lean
  • In a dense stand, a neighbour's branch is easily mistaken for the subject tree's crown base
  • Slope distance used instead of horizontal distance inflates every height on the sheet

Section A · Field procedure — and the bridge into Section B

Crown diameter and crown projection area

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit III · Session 5

The eye is a poor plumb line. Finding the ground point vertically below the crown edge is the hardest easy measurement in mensuration.

Procedure
1
Use a crown mirror — a levelled 45° mirror that lets you sight vertically upward while looking ahead.
2
Walk outward until the crown edge sits on the cross-hair. Mark the ground point.
3
Tape from that point back to the centre of the stem — that is one crown radius.
4
Repeat on at least two perpendicular axes (or four to eight radii for research work).
5
Average the diameters, then compute the projection area.
CPA  =  π  ( CD̄ / 2 )2 CD̄ = mean crown diameter · CPA = crown projection area, in m2
Two perpendicular diameters · averaged · treated as a circle
Know the bias you are accepting

A circle fills in the bays between the lobes, so it overestimates the area of a lobed crown. Acceptable for a stand cover figure; not acceptable for research on individual crowns, where the crown is mapped as a polygon.

Section A · The derived variable foresters actually use

Live crown ratio and tree vigour

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit III · Session 5

Crown length expressed as a percentage of total height. It is dimensionless, which is what makes a sapling comparable with a mature tree.

LCR  =  ( CL / H ) × 100 crown length ÷ total height, as a percentage
Long crown
LCR > 50 %

Large factory, small stem to feed. Vigorous, responds well to release, wind-firm.

Moderate
30 – 50 %

Normal for a managed even-aged stand approaching first thinning. Watch it.

Short crown
LCR < 30 %

Long-shaded, branches shed. Poor response to release; at risk of snow break and windthrow.

Crown ratio is not recoverable in conifers

A shed branch does not grow back. A thinning delayed until the crop trees are at 20 % has been delayed too long — thinning is preventive, not curative.

Why this belongs in a session on closure

Closure counts area covered, not depth of crown behind it. Two stands with identical closure can differ completely in vigour.

Section A · Kraft's classification, 1884 — and why it is on this slide

Crown classes in the canopy

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit III · Session 5
Dominant

Crown above the general canopy level. Light from above and all sides.

Co-dominant

Crown forms the general canopy level. Light from above only; crowded at the sides.

Intermediate

Squeezed into the canopy. A little light from above, none from the side.

Suppressed

Entirely below the canopy. Only filtered light. Also called overtopped.

Why you needed this before Section B

Crown closure is defined on dominant and co-dominant crowns — the trees that form the canopy. A densiometer, however, cannot tell one crown class from another, and counts intermediates too. That alone makes the two numbers differ.

Worked example 1 · the dimensions of one crown

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit III · Session 5

A blue pine (Pinus wallichiana) is measured. Total height 24.6 m; bole height 9.8 m; crown diameter 6.4 m north–south and 5.2 m east–west. Find the crown length, live crown ratio, crown height, mean crown diameter and crown projection area.

Given
H  = 24.6 m
BH = 9.8 m
CDNS = 6.4 m   CDEW = 5.2 m
1 · Crown length
CL = H − BH = 24.6 − 9.8 = 14.8 m
2 · Live crown ratio
LCR = (14.8 / 24.6) × 100 = 0.60163 × 100 = 60.2 %
3 · Crown height
CH = BH + CL/2 = 9.8 + 7.4 = 17.2 m
4 · Mean crown diameter
CD̄ = (6.4 + 5.2) / 2 = 5.8 m
5 · Projection area
CPA = π (5.8/2)2 = π × 2.92 = π × 8.41 = 26.42 m2
Answer 14.8 m · 60.2 % · 17.2 m · 26.42 m2
Two errors the marker expects to see

Dividing crown length by bole height in step 2, and forgetting to add bole height back in step 3 — which gives 7.4 m instead of 17.2 m.

Interpretation — never leave a number unread

A live crown ratio of 60 % on a blue pine is a vigorous tree with a full crown: a good crop tree, worth marking to retain at the next thinning. The two crown diameters differ by more than 20 %, which is normal where a tree has a close neighbour on one side — and is exactly why two perpendicular axes are the minimum.

Three definitions that are not the same thing

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit III · Session 5
Crown Closure
VIEWED HORIZONTALLY

The proportion of forest area where the crowns of dominant and co-dominant trees touch or overlap when viewed horizontally.

Canopy Cover  /  Crown Cover
VIEWED FROM ABOVE

The percentage of ground area covered by the vertical projection of tree crowns, including all vegetation layers (trees, shrubs, and so on).

Canopy Closure
VIEWED FROM BELOW

The proportion of the sky obscured by foliage when viewed from the forest floor.

The structure that makes them memorable

They differ in where the observer stands and which way they look: crowns against each other, horizontally · the ground seen from directly above · the sky seen from the forest floor. Three viewpoints, three numbers.

Section B · Definition 1, unpacked

Crown closure — are the crowns joined up?

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit III · Session 5

Three phrases in this definition do real work — and each of them excludes something.

"of dominant and co-dominant trees"

Excludes the understorey. A dense layer of suppressed saplings or shrubs adds nothing to crown closure, however green the plot looks.

"touch or overlap"

A statement about neighbouring crowns, not about ground shaded. This is what makes it a closure measure rather than a cover measure.

"when viewed horizontally"

Describes the canopy as a continuous horizontal sheet — whether it is joined up — not the shadows it casts on the ground.

Closure describes the continuity of the canopy layer
The silvicultural reading

"The stand has closed canopy" is a milestone in stand development: after it, competition begins, height growth accelerates, diameter growth slows, and self-pruning starts. Thinning is timed against it.

Section B · Definition 2, unpacked

Canopy cover — how much ground is shaded at noon?

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit III · Session 5

Vertical projection is the whole definition. Everything else is detail.

Cover  =  ( Acovered / Aground ) × 100 area of ground under crown projections ÷ total ground area
Crown cover or canopy cover?
  • Crown cover is used in tree-level studies, where each tree's crown is mapped or measured individually
  • Canopy cover is broader — used in stand-level and landscape assessment, and may include understorey vegetation depending on the protocol
  • Because the view is strictly vertical and downward, this is the quantity that aerial photographs, satellite imagery and lidar naturally measure
The rule students forget under pressure

Canopy cover can never exceed 100 %. Ground is either covered or it is not; covering it twice does not cover it more.

Cover is the shadow at noon, counted once

Section B · Definition 3, unpacked — the intellectual centre of the session

Canopy closure — how much sky is blocked?

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit III · Session 5

Lie on your back on the forest floor. You see a hemisphere of sky, partly blocked by foliage. The fraction blocked is canopy closure.

It is an angular measure, not an area measure
  • From a point on the ground you see not only the crown above you but crowns 15, 20, 30 m away, viewed obliquely
  • Viewed obliquely a crown blocks far more of your angular field than its vertical projection would suggest — you look through its side, not down through its depth
  • Therefore closure measured from a point is almost always larger than cover over the same stand
  • The figure depends entirely on the angle of view of the instrument used
The consequence you must be able to state

A densiometer reading and a lidar cover figure for the same stand will not agree — and neither is wrong. They are measuring different quantities. Always report the angle of view with the figure.

The wider the accepted cone, the larger the closure figure
Angle of view decides the answer
narrow cone about vertical  →  converges on cover
convex densiometer, ≈ 60°  →  intermediate
full hemisphere, 180°  →  largest closure

The rule, and its caveat

On most stands canopy closure > crown closure > canopy cover. In a very open stand the three converge, because there is little oblique blocking and little overlap. They diverge most in tall, dense stands — where the measurement matters most.

Section B · Where the umbrella analogy becomes arithmetic

Crown cover, overlap and canopy cover

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit III · Session 5
1 · Crown cover — the naive sum
CCcrown = ( Σ CPAi / A ) × 100

Add up every crown projection area and divide by plot area. This can exceed 100 % — and when it does it is not an error, it is information: the crowns overlap.

2 · Canopy cover — overlap counted once
CCcanopy = ( ( Σ CPAi − Aoverlap ) / A ) × 100

The only honest way to obtain Aoverlap is to map the crowns — on a plan sheet, on an aerial photograph, or in GIS. This figure cannot exceed 100 %.

3 · If crowns were placed at random
CCcanopy = 100 ( 1 − e−Σ CPA / A )

The chance a point is covered by no crown falls off exponentially. A model, not a measurement — it assumes crowns are small relative to the plot and independently placed.

Plan view of crown projections on a sample plot
The residual is a statement about spatial pattern

Measured cover below the random model → crowns are clumped. Measured cover above it → crowns are more regularly spaced than chance, which is what a plantation looks like.

Worked example 2 · crown cover, overlap and canopy cover

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit III · Session 5

On a 20 m × 20 m plot, 12 trees are measured. The crown projection areas sum to 268 m2. The crowns are mapped on a plan sheet and the total overlapped area is found to be 54 m2. Find crown cover and canopy cover, and compare with the random-placement model.

Given
A   = 20 × 20 = 400 m2
Σ CPA = 268 m2
Aoverlap = 54 m2
1 · Crown cover
(268 / 400) × 100 = 0.670 × 100 = 67.0 %   — could legitimately exceed 100 %
2 · Ground actually covered
268 − 54 = 214 m2
3 · Canopy cover
(214 / 400) × 100 = 0.535 × 100 = 53.5 %
4 · Random model
100 (1 − e−268/400) = 100 (1 − e−0.67) = 100 (1 − 0.51171) = 48.8 %
Answer 67.0 %  ·  53.5 % crown cover · canopy cover
Neither figure is wrong

67.0 % and 53.5 % are different quantities, not a measurement and an error. Report which one you mean.

Interpretation

Measured canopy cover (53.5 %) is above the random prediction (48.8 %). More cover means less overlap than chance, so the crowns are more evenly spaced than a random scatter — the signature of a plantation, or of a stand that has been thinned. A measured 42 % would have implied the opposite: clumping, from group regeneration or a patchy site.

Section C · Method 1 — the most used method in the world

Ocular estimation from the forest floor

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit III · Session 5

A trained eye is genuinely good. An untrained eye is genuinely bad. The difference between them is calibration.

Three documented biases
  • Overestimation below ~30 % — scattered crowns look more numerous than they are
  • Underestimation above ~70 % — small gaps are conspicuous and attract more attention than they deserve
  • Rounding to multiples of ten — a set of ocular estimates spikes at 50, 60, 70 and contains almost no odd values
Four fixes, all cheap
  • Estimate a class, not a point value — a class states the precision that actually exists
  • Calibrate against a densiometer or a photograph at the start of the day, and again after lunch
  • Two observers estimate independently; reconcile only if they differ by more than one class
  • Record who estimated — between-observer differences are real and persistent
The characteristic S-shaped observer bias
Why it survives despite all of that

It costs nothing and takes ten seconds. A hundred rough estimates spread over a compartment often describe it better than four excellent ones taken where the crew happened to stand.

The spherical densiometer

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit III · Session 5
Convex spherical densiometer · angle of view ≈ 60°
What the instrument is
  • A small box holding a curved mirror with a grid of 24 squares engraved on it, plus a spirit level
  • The convex version — Lemmon (1956), and the common one — reflects roughly 60° of sky; the concave version reflects a narrower field
  • The 24 squares are not the counting unit — the observer imagines four equally spaced dots in each square
96 dots  →  100 / 96  =  1.0417  ≈  1.04 % per dot the origin of the 1.04 factor — it is not a magic number
The convention — and the error that follows from mixing conventions
count dots showing open sky  →  × 1.04 = % not occupied
canopy cover  =  100 − that figure

Counting covered dots directly is also in use. Both are correct if applied consistently; mixing them gives a reading of 20 % inside a closed forest.

The honest limitation

A wide angle of view means the convex densiometer does not measure vertical canopy cover. It measures something closer to canopy closure and reads higher than vertical methods — which is what the instrument does, not a fault in it.

Densiometer · the field procedure

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit III · Session 5
Procedure at one sample point
1
Hold the instrument level — use the spirit level — at about elbow height, 30–45 cm in front of the body.
2
Position your head so that its reflection falls just outside the grid. A head inside the grid is counted as canopy, and biases every reading upward.
3
Imagine four equally spaced dots in each of the 24 squares — 96 dots in all.
4
Count the dots showing open sky. Record the raw count.
5
Repeat facing north, east, south and west. A canopy is directional; one reading does not describe it.
6
Mean the four counts, multiply by 1.04 for the percentage not occupied, and subtract from 100.
7
Repeat at several points per plot — commonly the centre plus four points on the cardinal bearings.
Point layout · four bearings at each point
Two cautions and one rule
  • Avoid direct sunlight — glare makes the canopy–sky boundary unjudgeable. Overcast, early morning or late afternoon is best
  • Where possible, one observer takes all readings for a plot; dot counting is subjective at the edges and observers differ consistently
  • Record the raw counts, not only the percentage. A raw count can be re-checked; a percentage cannot

Hemispherical photography

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit III · Session 5
1
Capture
  • Levelled tripod, oriented to magnetic north
  • Uniformly overcast sky, or dawn or dusk — direct sun in the frame is read as sky where it should be canopy
2
Threshold
  • Everything downstream depends on where the threshold is placed
  • Leaf-edge pixels are genuinely ambiguous — use an automatic algorithm and report which one
  • A hand-chosen threshold is an unrecorded decision nobody can reproduce
3
Measure by ring
  • Report closure over any cone you choose — the whole hemisphere, the central 60° to compare with a densiometer, or a narrow cone to approximate vertical cover
  • The same image also yields leaf area index and the seasonal light regime
The real advantage

One photograph answers several questions, and is a permanent, re-analysable record. No other field method leaves evidence behind.

A note on smartphones

A clip-on fisheye is not a calibrated lens — but it is far better than nothing, and entirely adequate for teaching and for relative comparisons.

Section C · Method 4 — objective because every decision is yes or no

Transects, tubes and grids

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit III · Session 5
Line intercept
length ÷ length

Run a tape along a straight line and record the length lying beneath crown projections. Cover = intercepted length / total length × 100. Slow, needs a vertical sighting device — and it is the method against which many others are validated.

Point intercept · sighting tube
hits ÷ points

A levelled tube with a cross-hair, sighted vertically at regular points along a transect. Hit or miss at each. The standard error follows from binomial theory, so the number of points needed for a stated precision can be computed in advance.

Moosehorn
narrow angle

A periscope-like tube with a grid of dots viewed against the canopy, sighted vertically. Because its angle of view is narrow, unlike the densiometer it genuinely approximates vertical canopy cover.

Canopy scope  ·  dot grid
dots ÷ dots

A transparent sheet of dots held at arm's length, or laid over an aerial photograph. This is how cover was measured over large areas before digital imagery — and modern pixel counting is simply a very fine dot grid.

The shared virtue

No judgement of "how much" — only a series of yes-or-no decisions, which are far more repeatable between observers than proportion estimates.

Worth knowing when the budget is tight

A canopy scope and a sighting tube can be built in the department workshop this afternoon from a transparent sheet and a length of pipe.

Section C · Method 5 — where the national forest cover figure comes from

Remote sensing and lidar

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit III · Session 5
Aerial photographs and satellite imagery
  • A downward view, so what they naturally measure is canopy cover, not closure
  • High resolution: individual crowns delineated and their areas summed
  • Coarse resolution: cover estimated from the spectral mixture within a pixel — a 30 m pixel contains canopy, shade and ground together
  • At that scale the cover figure is a model output, not a direct measurement. Say so when reporting it
Airborne lidar — different in kind

Laser pulses are fired downward and the height of each return recorded.

Cover = first returns above 2 m ÷ all first returns

A direct geometric ratio corresponding almost exactly to the vertical-projection definition — the closest thing to an unbiased cover measurement that exists.

Lidar first returns above and below the height threshold
The professional point — say this one twice

Remote sensing does not remove the need for field measurement; it changes its purpose. Field plots become the calibration and validation data. A national forest cover figure is a remote product checked against plots measured with densiometers and tapes — which is why this session still matters in an age of satellites.

The national procedure

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit III · Session 5
The instrument, and the reading
  • GRS crown densitometer — a sighting tube with cross-hairs and levelling bubbles, not the convex mirror of slide 35
  • Level the bubble, sight vertically, read the inner black circle
  • Score 1 if more than half that circle is filled by canopy, 0 if it is not
  • One point, one digit. No dot count, no mean of four bearings, no × 1.04
The plot
  • Circular, radius 12.62 m — an area of 500 m2
  • 100 observation points on a 2.5 m × 2.5 m grid
  • 89 systematic points and 11 random — and the random ones are taken from the same quadrant in every plot
  • The grid is laid out in GIS. The observer never chooses where to stand
The arithmetic
Cover %  =  ( Σ 1s / 100 ) × 100
  • Across several plots the divisor is 100 × number of plots
  • Where more than 100 points are read, the divisor is the number of points — not 100
  • Every reading is a whole percentage point, which is why a hundred of them are taken

Sources of error and good practice

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit III · Session 5
Random error — more readings fix it
  • Judging a dot at the edge of a leaf as sky or canopy
  • Small shifts in where the observer stands between readings
  • Wind moving foliage between bearings
  • Reading a tape or a clinometer to the nearest whole unit
Property

Scatters around the true value. Averaging many readings brings the mean towards the truth.

Systematic error — more readings do not
SourceDirection
Observer's head reflected inside the gridtoo high
Instrument not held levelone-sided on slopes
Wide angle of view, compared with vertical covertoo high
Deciduous stand read in leaf-off seasontoo low
Understorey counted when the protocol wanted trees onlytoo high
Points placed where walking is easy — along roads, away from thicketsusually too low
Property

Sits to one side of the truth. A thousand readings taken with the head inside the grid give a thousand readings that are all too high.

Good practice — every item on this list is free

Level the instrument · keep your reflection outside the grid · read on four bearings · use several points per plot · place points by a rule, not by convenience · avoid direct sun · one observer per plot · record raw counts and the method · state the season. If you could remove one error, remove the sampling bias: instrument error is small and bounded; sampling bias is neither.

Summary of Session 5

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit III · Session 5
A
Terms related to crown
  • The crown is the upper branchy part above the bole; the canopy is what many crowns form together
  • Crown base is the lowest live branch; bole height runs from the ground up to it
  • Crown length = H − BH, and crown height = BH + CL/2 in this module's convention
  • Three conventions for "crown height" exist — state the one you used
  • Crown diameter is averaged over at least two perpendicular axes, then CPA = π(CD̄/2)2
  • LCR = CL/H × 100 indexes vigour; in conifers it is not recoverable once lost
  • Crown closure is defined on dominant and co-dominant crowns — hence Kraft's classes
B
Crown closure
  • Crown closure — crowns of dominant and co-dominant trees touching or overlapping, viewed horizontally
  • Canopy cover / crown cover — ground covered by the vertical projection of crowns, all layers
  • Canopy closure — the share of sky obscured by foliage, from the forest floor
  • Three viewpoints: crowns against each other · the ground from above · the sky from below
  • Cover is areal; closure is angular and depends on angle of view
  • On most stands closure > crown closure > cover; in open stands they converge
  • CC = ((ΣCPA − Aov)/A) × 100; under random placement CC = 100(1 − e−ΣCPA/A)
C
Measuring canopy closure
  • Ocular estimation is free and biased — calibrate, use classes, record the observer
  • Classes: 0–2 almost no trees · 2–10 sparse · 10–40 open · 40–80 dense · 80–100 close forest
  • Densiometer: 24 squares × 4 imagined dots = 96, so one dot = 1.04 %
  • Count open-sky dots, × 1.04, then subtract from 100. Four bearings per point
  • Hemispherical photography gives closure over any chosen cone, plus LAI, plus a permanent record
  • Lidar cover = first returns above 2 m ÷ all first returns — the nearest thing to unbiased cover
  • A figure without its method is not a measurement
