Section 1Learning outcomes

  1. Define basal area and compute it for a tree and for a stand by three methods.
  2. Distinguish crop diameter, mean diameter and top diameter, and explain why crop diameter is always the larger.
  3. Compute Lorey's crop height and mean height, and say which is wanted when.
  4. Compute the mean age of an uneven-aged crop by five methods.
  5. Expand a sample plot to a per-hectare and a whole-forest figure, with correct units.
  6. Estimate biomass by both routes, choose the right equation, and convert to a per-hectare and carbon figure.
  7. Compute a mean, variance, standard error and confidence interval from plot data, and say what one plot cannot tell you.

Section 2Basal area

Basal area
The cross-sectional area of a stem at breast height. For one tree, g; for a stand, G, in m²/ha.
g = π d² / 4   ·   g (m²) = 0.00007854 × d² (d in cm)

Four uses

  1. Stand density. Basal area measures crowding in a way that reflects both tree size and tree number — which is the whole argument for it.
  2. Stand volume. V = G × h × f — the sentence that joins Unit V to Unit VI. G from this unit, h from Section 4 below, f from Unit V.
  3. Stand growth. Increment is the difference between two basal-area measurements on the same plot at two dates. Basal-area increment is easier to measure reliably than volume increment, because it needs only diameters.
  4. Forest management. Stands are thinned back to a target basal area rather than a target number of stems, precisely because the target then means the same thing in a stand of big trees and a stand of small ones.
Two stands, same basal area

Doubling the diameter quarters the number of trees needed for the same basal area, because g goes with d². So 30 m²/ha can be 1 200 small stems or 150 large ones. Basal area alone does not describe structure — which is why crop diameter and the diameter distribution matter.

Section 3Three ways to get G

1 · Summing tree basal areas in a plot

Measure every DBH in a plot of known area, compute g for each, sum, and expand.

G (m²/ha) = Σg ÷ plot area in ha
Worked example 1 · stand basal area from a plot

A 0.05 ha plot (500 m²) holds 24 stems whose basal areas total 1.6084 m².

Expansion factor1 ÷ 0.05 = 20
G1.6084 × 20 = 32.17 m²/ha
G ≈ 32.2 m²/ha

2 · Point sampling

Stand at a point. Sweep a full circle through a gauge of fixed angle. Count every stem that looks wider than the gauge. Multiply.

G = N × BAF N = trees counted · BAF = basal area factor of the gauge, m²/ha per tree
Why it works — the sentence to be able to repeat back

A tree is counted only if it is closer to the point than a limiting distance that is proportional to its diameter. So each tree carries its own circular plot, and the size of that plot grows with the square of diameter — exactly as basal area does. The two effects cancel, so every counted tree, big or small, contributes the same basal area per hectare. That amount is the basal area factor.

Worked example 2 · point sampling

Eight points on a systematic grid, gauge BAF 2 m²/ha per tree. Counts: 12, 15, 11, 14, 13, 16, 12, 15.

Total108
Mean per point108 ÷ 8 = 13.5
G13.5 × 2 = 27.0 m²/ha
G = 27.0 m²/ha

The borderline tree is the weak point. Crews unconsciously include big trees and exclude small ones, and that error is systematic — it does not average out. Where a tree looks borderline, measure its diameter and distance and decide by the rule.

3 · The spacing factor

The spacing factor F is the average distance between trees divided by the mean diameter, both in the same units — also called relative spacing.

F = s ÷ d   (s in m, d in m)

The derivation, in four lines:

  1. Trees per hectare on a square lattice: N = 10 000 / s²
  2. Substitute s = F d: N = 10 000 / (F² d²)
  3. Basal area: G = N × π d² / 4
  4. The d² cancels: G = 10 000 π / (4 F²) = 7 854 / F²
G = 7 854 / F² the surprise: G depends only on the relative spacing, not on tree size at all
Worked example 3 · spacing factor

Mean spacing paced at 4.5 m; mean DBH 25 cm.

F4.5 ÷ 0.25 = 18
G7 854 ÷ 18² = 7 854 ÷ 324 = 24.2 m²/ha
G ≈ 24.2 m²/ha

Calibrate your pace. F sits in the denominator squared: a pace 5 % short reports a spacing 5 % short and a basal area about 10 % high. Errors in spacing are magnified.

Where it earns its place: a district forester walking a chir pine stand can pace the spacing, take three or four diameters, and have a basal area figure inside five minutes without laying out a single plot. Not accurate enough for a sale; entirely adequate for deciding whether a stand needs thinning this year or next.

Section 4Diameter of the crop

The central idea

Crop diameter is based on the mean basal area — not on the mean of the diameters.

Crop diameter
The diameter corresponding to the mean basal area of a uniform, generally pure crop.
Mean diameter
The diameter corresponding to the mean basal area of a group of trees or a stand. A general term, used for any stand structure.
Arithmetic mean diameter
Simply Σ(n·d)/Σn. Not the crop diameter.
Top diameter
The diameter corresponding to the mean basal area of the 250 biggest diameters per hectare.
mean basal area ḡ = (π/4) × (crop diameter)²  →  D = √(4ḡ / π)
Worked example 4 · the two answers, and why they differ

Five plots totalling 0.25 ha, 185 trees in six diameter classes. Σ(n·g) = 7.4995 m²; Σ(n·d) = 37.54 m.

Arithmetic mean diameter37.54 ÷ 185 = 0.20292 m = 20.29 cm
Mean basal area7.4995 ÷ 185 = 0.040538 m²
Crop diameter√(4 × 0.040538 ÷ π) = √0.051615 = 0.22720 m = 22.72 cm
Crop diameter is 2.43 cm — about 12 % — larger
The crop diameter is always the larger

Squaring gives the big stems more weight than counting them does. Averaging the diameters treats a 10 cm sapling and a 45 cm veteran as equals; averaging the basal areas does not. The gap widens as the diameter distribution becomes more skewed, and closes in a uniform even-aged plantation.

Worked example 5 · top diameter

Same dataset, 0.25 ha. Fill the quota from the largest classes down.

Quota250/ha × 0.25 ha = 62.5 trees
Fill from the top8 (45 cm) + 13 (38) + 25 (31) = 46 → take 16.5 more at 24 cm
Their basal area1.272345 + 1.474349 + 1.886919 + 16.5 × 0.045239 = 5.38006 m²
Mean basal area5.38006 ÷ 62.5 = 0.086081 m²
Top diameter√(4 × 0.086081 ÷ π) = 0.33106 m
Top diameter = 33.11 cm

Why 250, and why the biggest? Because the largest trees are the least affected by thinning history and stocking, and therefore the best available signal of what the site can produce. Top diameter feeds top height, and top height feeds site quality.

Section 5Height of the crop

Lorey's crop height

HL = (s₁h₁ + s₂h₂ + …) ÷ (s₁ + s₂ + …) s = the total basal area of each diameter class · h = the mean height of that class

It is a basal-area weighted mean height. Big trees count for more, in exact proportion to the wood they carry — because a stand's volume is dominated by its large trees, so its representative height must be too.

The plain arithmetic mean height, weighted by number of trees, is 14.31 m.

The consequence — and how to state it

Use 14.31 m in V = G × h × f and the crop volume is understated by 22.6 % of the correct figure: (18.49 − 14.31) ÷ 18.49. On a 50 ha coupe that is not a rounding error.

Dividing by 14.31 instead gives 29.2 %, which answers a different question — how much the wrong figure would have to rise, not how much of the true value it misses. Be clear which you are quoting.

The gap is wide here because the stand is reverse-J: three fifths of the trees sit in the two smallest classes, but those classes hold only 21 % of the basal area. In a uniform even-aged plantation the two figures would be much closer.

Mean height and top height

Mean height is read from the height–diameter curve at the crop diameter. Two readings of one curve — at the crop diameter and at the arithmetic mean diameter — give different heights, and the difference is the same lesson as above.

Top height is the height corresponding to the mean diameter, calculated from basal area, of the 250 biggest diameters per hectare, read from the height–diameter curve.

The contrast to hold on to

Crop height and mean height relate to all the trees of the crop. Top height relates to only the 250 biggest. Site quality uses top height because it is largely independent of stocking and thinning history — two stands of the same species and age with the same top height have the same productive capacity, however differently they have been managed.

Section 6Age of the crop

For an even-aged crop, the age of the crop is the age of its trees. For an uneven-aged crop there are five methods, and they give different answers because they weight by different things.

Section 7Volume of the crop

Direct measurement of the volume of a whole stand is neither practical nor economical — nobody fells a forest to find out how much wood it holds. Volume is built up from parts: a sample, a table or an equation, and an expansion.

Vtotal = (Vsample ÷ sample area) × total area

Route 1 · with a volume table. Read volume per tree straight from the table by diameter class. No height or form factor needed — they are built into the table. This is what a field forester does.

Route 2 · without one. Compute V = G × h × f. This is what an examination asks for when no table is given.

Worked example 8 · expanding a sample plot volume

Sample plot 0.1 ha; volume of all trees in it 5 m³; total forest area 50 ha.

Per hectare5 ÷ 0.1 = 50 m³/ha
Expand50 × 50 = 2 500
2 500 m³ — total standing volume of the 50 ha forest

The unit, and why it is not m³/ha. 50 m³/ha × 50 ha — the hectares cancel, leaving cubic metres. A total is m³; only the intermediate figure is per hectare. Catching this kind of slip in other people's work is a professional skill.

The real weakness of that estimate — and the wrong version of the criticism

It is one plot. One plot cannot represent the spatial variability of 50 ha, and it gives no design-based variance, so the figure cannot honestly be quoted with a standard error or a confidence interval.

Note what the weakness is not. The sampling fraction here is 0.2 %, and a small sampling fraction is not by itself evidence of poor precision — a national inventory samples a far smaller fraction and is far more precise. Precision depends on the number of plots, their distribution, the variability of the population, the design, the plot size, the estimator, measurement quality and spatial autocorrelation.

Section 8Biomass and carbon

Two routes

Route 1 · indirect, through volume.

B = V × ρ × BEF volume (m³) × wood density (kg/m³) × biomass expansion factor

Use it when you already hold a volume figure and no tree list. The cost: three uncertainties stacked — volume error, a density that is usually a species or regional average, and an expansion factor that is usually a national default.

Route 2 · direct, allometric.

B = f (DBH)  or  f (DBH, h) no volume is computed at all

Use it when you hold tree measurements — which, after Units II and IV, you always do. The cost: only the equation's own error. This is the route Bhutan's National Forest Inventory and every Bhutanese carbon report uses.

The selection hierarchy — work down it, never up

  1. Species-specific, DBH and height, when the species is one of the fourteen and the tree lies inside the fitted DBH range.
  2. Species-specific, DBH only, when reliable height is not available.
  3. General conifer or general broadleaf — only when no suitable species equation exists.

Record all six, every time: equation source and year; species it was fitted to; input variables and their units; calibration range (n, min and max DBH); output unit; and why this equation and not another.

Structure of the Bhutan equations

B = b₀ + b₁·BA + b₂·X₂   or   B = b₀ + b₁·BAH + b₂·X₂ BA = πd²/4 in square metres; BAH = BA × h in cubic metres; B in kilograms

X₂ is a restricted cubic spline of the predictor, with the same form as the volume models in Unit V. The knots are the 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles of the predictor in the fitting data, and they differ between the BA form and the BAH form of the same species.

Five ways to get a plausible-looking wrong answer

  1. Omitting the spline term because it looks like a correction. Drop it from the DBH-only equation for a 45 cm blue pine and the estimate falls from 740 kg to 546 kg.
  2. Mixing centimetres and metres, or cm² and m², in the predictor.
  3. Using a tree outside the fitted DBH range without saying so.
  4. Taking a general equation when a species equation exists.
  5. Assuming equation error disappears on aggregation.
On the fifth — say it precisely

Positive and negative random errors may partly offset in a sufficiently large sample. Shared model bias, site effects, species differences, measurement bias and extrapolation error do not offset — they survive summation intact and scale with the estimate. A stand total is not automatically more accurate than the trees it was built from.

Tree biomass to stand biomass to carbon

  1. Compute biomass for every tree individually — never from a mean tree.
  2. Sum within the plot (kg/plot).
  3. Divide by plot area in hectares — for a 500 m² plot, ÷ 0.05, the same as × 20.
  4. Report kg/ha, then convert: 1 Mg = 1 000 kg = 1 tonne.
  5. Report Mg/ha — the unit every national and international forest-carbon report uses.
  6. Convert to carbon only with the fraction the applicable protocol requires, and cite that protocol beside the figure.
Do not memorise a carbon fraction

The biomass-to-carbon fraction is set by the reporting protocol, and it differs between protocols and between carbon pools. The examinable skill is finding and citing the applicable fraction — not reciting a number.

Why biomass cannot be taken from the mean tree

The rule that reaches far past this unit

You may take an average before applying a linear function. You may never take an average before applying a curved one. The biomass equation is a cubic in basal area, so the biomass of the mean tree multiplied by the number of trees is not the biomass of the stand.

On a 12-tree plot the difference is 3 493 kg computed tree by tree against 2 714 kg from the mean tree — 22 % low, because the one 60 cm tree carries almost half the biomass on its own. This is why an inventory records individual trees and not class totals.

Trees A and B: same DBH, so the DBH-only equation must give them the same biomass

It cannot do anything else — height is not in the equation. But B is six metres taller, and the height-inclusive equation puts it 37 % heavier than A.

And the subtler point. The two totals differ by only 4.4 %, because A's overestimate and B's underestimate partly cancel. Do not conclude that the diameter-only equation is therefore fine for stands. It cancelled here because this little sample happened to contain one short tree and one tall one of the same diameter. In a stand systematically taller or shorter than the fitting data — a fertile valley bottom, an exposed ridge — the errors all point the same way and nothing cancels.

Section 9Sampling and inference

The frame

The target population is what you want to describe — all forest in the FMU. The sampling population is what your frame can actually reach. They are never identical, and every exclusion — an out-of-date boundary, a cliff, a restricted area, a plot nobody could get to — moves the second away from the first. That gap is where bias lives, and it does not show up anywhere in the arithmetic.

Write down before the crew leaves: the forest boundary used and its source and date; what is excluded and on what rule; what will be done about inaccessible plots; and whether the frame is known to be incomplete.

Four designs

DesignWhat it is
Simple randomEvery location equally likely. The reference case for the mathematics — and expensive to walk.
SystematicA grid. Spreads plots evenly and is easy to navigate to. Bhutan's NFI uses a 4 × 4 km grid.
StratifiedSplit the forest into types first, sample each. Buys precision when the strata really do differ.
ClusterSeveral plots at each visited location. What you do when travel dominates cost — in Bhutan, three plots per cluster.

Two further axes, often confused with the design: fixed-area plots take every tree inside a boundary, while variable-radius plots take a tree with probability proportional to its basal area; and permanent plots are re-measured while temporary ones are not. Only permanent plots can give increment — everything in Unit VII depends on them existing.

Expansion factors

expansion factor = 1 ha ÷ plot area in ha  ·  for 500 m² = 0.05 ha, EF = 20

Three things to take from that interval. It is wide — the honest statement about this stand is a range, not the number 200. Divide by n − 1, because the mean was estimated from the same data. And SE falls with the square root of n: four times the plots halves it. That one sentence governs every inventory budget ever written.

How many plots would you actually need?
Coefficient of variationCV = 51.9 ÷ 200 = 26 %
For a 10 % relative SEn = (26 ÷ 10)² ≈ 7 plots
Seven plots of 0.05 ha in 50 ha is a sampling fraction of 0.7 %

What buys precision is the number of plots set against the variability of the forest — not the fraction of the area covered.

Two errors, not one

Measurement error is the error in reading one tree, reduced by calibration, training and protocol. Sampling error is the error from having measured plots instead of the whole forest, reduced by more plots. A defensible report accounts for both — and, after the biomass section, for model error as well.

Inaccessible plots and non-response

  • Record it — coordinates, date, and the reason it could not be measured.
  • Replace only under a written rule agreed before fieldwork, and record that a replacement was used.
  • Never walk on and measure the nearest convenient stand instead.
Why substitution is the most damaging thing a crew can do

Steep, remote and cliff-bound ground is not a random subset of the forest — it carries different stocking, different species and different disturbance history. Systematically dropping it biases every estimate the inventory produces, in a direction nobody can afterwards calculate. A documented gap can be adjusted for. An undocumented substitution cannot.

Section 10Error and quality control

Random — reduced by measuring more: natural variation between plots, small reading differences, borderline judgements.

Systematic — not reduced by measuring more: a stretched or worn girth tape (reads high on every tree); a crew measuring downhill on slopes (high on every tree); generous inclusion of borderline trees in point sampling (every plot upward); a form factor from another species or site (every volume).

The QA/QC list

  • Calibrate tapes, callipers and clinometers at the start of each season.
  • Repeat measurements on a sample of trees, blind.
  • State the tolerance a repeat must fall within.
  • Species-code checks against the agreed list.
  • Coordinate checks — is the plot where it should be?
  • Range checks on every field before analysis. A DBH of 400 cm is a typing error; a height of 3 m against a 60 cm stem is a transposition. Neither is visible once the data are summed.
  • Duplicate-tree prevention in permanent plots.
  • Field-team consistency — the same rules across crews.
  • Supervisor re-measurement of a proportion of plots.
  • Data validation before any analysis begins.

Every departure from the written method — a shifted measurement height, a different instrument, a plot moved — is recorded at the time, not reconstructed later.

The question to expect

“Your data give 200 m³/ha. Who checked it, when, against what tolerance, and where is that recorded?” A number without that context is not a result; it is a rumour.

Section 11In Bhutanese forestry

Every figure in this unit ends up in a document somebody has to defend.

  • Sustainable yield. Basal area and growing stock per hectare set the allowable cut in an FMU working plan and in a community-forest management plan.
  • Carbon reporting. Biomass per hectare, from the 2018 national equations, feeds the greenhouse-gas inventory and Bhutan's carbon-neutrality commitment.
  • National reporting. Growing stock and forest condition are published in the State of Forest Report and in Bhutan's international submissions.
  • Crop structure. Diameter-class distribution, top height and site quality decide silvicultural treatment — thinning, regeneration felling, or leaving well alone.
Community forestry

The arithmetic of this unit is the arithmetic of a community-forest management plan: prepare and revise the plan; estimate the group's own timber, fuelwood and pole requirement against what the forest can sustainably supply; monitor regeneration; identify culturally important species and assess NWFP resources; and combine members' local ecological knowledge with plot measurement — not one instead of the other — in support of transparent and equitable allocation within the group.

Beyond timber — the same instruments

Large habitat trees, standing and fallen deadwood, diameter-class diversity, crown condition, regeneration density, basal area by species group, biomass and carbon, canopy cover, riparian stand structure, climate-related mortality, change between inventories. Not one of these is volume, and every one is measured with the instruments of this module. Volume is one output of mensuration, not its purpose.

Section 12Practice questions

  1. Define basal area and state its four uses.
  2. A 0.04 ha plot holds stems whose basal areas total 1.02 m². Compute G in m²/ha.
  3. Six point-sampling stations with a BAF of 4 give counts of 7, 9, 6, 8, 10 and 8. Estimate G.
  4. A stand has a mean spacing of 3.8 m and a mean DBH of 22 cm. Estimate G by the spacing factor, and state what a 5 % pacing error would do to the answer.
  5. Explain, with reference to squaring, why the crop diameter is always larger than the arithmetic mean diameter.
  6. From the Section 5 table, compute Lorey's crop height. Then compute the percentage by which the arithmetic mean height of 14.31 m understates it, and say which base you used and why.
  7. Distinguish crop height, mean height and top height, and say which one site quality uses and why.
  8. A 500 m² plot holds 3 493 kg of biomass. Express this in Mg/ha.
  9. Two blue pine both measure 30 cm DBH; one is 18 m tall and the other 25 m. What will the DBH-only equation say about their biomass, and why is that wrong?
  10. Four plots give 210, 185, 240 and 165 m³/ha. Compute the mean, the standard error and the 95 % confidence interval (t = 3.182 for 3 degrees of freedom).
  11. A crew cannot reach three of twenty plots and measures three easier ones nearby instead. What has this done to the estimate, and what should they have done?

Section 13Summary

  • g = πd²/4; G = Σg ÷ plot ha; G = N × BAF; G = 7 854/F².
  • Crop diameter comes from mean basal area and is always larger than the arithmetic mean diameter.
  • Top diameter and top height use the 250 biggest per hectare, because they are least affected by stocking — which is what makes them a site-quality signal.
  • Lorey's HL = Σ(s·h) ÷ Σs — basal-area weighted, because volume is dominated by large trees.
  • Mean age depends entirely on the weighting. State the method.
  • V = G × h × f; expand with EF = 1 ÷ plot ha; a total is m³, not m³/ha.
  • Biomass: two routes, a selection hierarchy, and tree by tree, never from the mean tree.
  • One plot gives no variance. SE falls with √n; precision comes from the number of plots against the variability of the forest, not from the sampling fraction.
