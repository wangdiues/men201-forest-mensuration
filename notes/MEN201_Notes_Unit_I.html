Section 1Learning outcomes

By the end of this unit you should be able to:

  2. Identify the four kinds of measurement it deals with — linear, area, volume and weight.
  3. Explain why volume carries so many management decisions, and name other quantities mensuration is asked to estimate.
  4. List the four objectives of forest mensuration and describe what each delivers.
  5. Frame the importance of mensuration as the practical questions it answers.
  6. Justify the claim that absolute accuracy is neither necessary nor desirable in forestry.
  7. Give the six reasons why only relative accuracy is attainable, named and explained, not merely listed.
  8. Distinguish precision from accuracy and classify a set of readings on both.

Section 2Key terms

Mensuration
From the Latin mensura, a measure. The branch of applied mathematics concerned with determining length, area and volume.
Forest mensuration
The determination of the dimensions, form, volume, age and increment of single trees, stands or whole woods, either standing or after felling.
Accuracy
Closeness of a measurement to the true value. Degraded by systematic error.
Precision
Closeness of repeated measurements to each other. Degraded by random error. A precise instrument may be consistently wrong.
Random error
Varies in size and sign from reading to reading, averages to about zero, and cancels as the number of readings grows. Costs precision. Cured by more readings, better technique, a finer instrument.
Systematic error (bias)
The same size and the same sign every time. It does not cancel — it survives averaging intact. Costs accuracy. Cured only by calibration, correction, or changing the procedure.
Growing stock
The volume of standing timber a forest holds at a given time.
Increment
The increase in girth, diameter, basal area, height, volume, quality, price or value of trees or crops during a given period. Developed fully in Unit VII.

Section 3Forest mensuration defined

Forest mensuration is the determination of the dimensions, form,
volume, age and increment of single trees, stands or whole woods,
either standing or after felling memorise this sentence in these words

Unpacking the definition

Four things are determined. Dimensions, form, volume, age and increment — read as four groups: how big, what shape, how much, and how fast.

Three scales. A single tree, a stand, or whole woods. The methods change with the scale: you can measure one tree exhaustively, a stand only by sampling, and whole woods only by sampling and expansion.

Two states. Standing or felled. This distinction runs through the whole module. A felled stem can be measured section by section and its volume obtained almost exactly; a standing tree's volume can only ever be estimated, because you cannot get at the middle of it.

The exponent matters more than the unit

Because area follows L² and volume follows roughly L², a small error in a diameter is not a small error downstream. A 1 % error in diameter is about a 2 % error in basal area and in every volume, biomass and carbon figure built on it. This single fact is the reason Unit II spends a fortnight on how to hold a tape.

Volume, and what sits beside it

Volume carries more management decisions than any other single variable: timber is traded in cubic metres, the annual allowable cut is a volume, growing stock and yield tables are volume statements, and increment is volume per hectare per year.

But volume is one variable, not the purpose of the discipline. The same measurements are asked to estimate:

growing stocktimber & fuelwood supplynon-wood forest productsbiomasscarbonforest structurebiodiversity habitatregenerationforest healthwatershed protectionlivelihood supplyrestorationsustainable harvestclimate-change impacts

Count how many of those fourteen are volume. One.

And volume is never measured on a standing tree

It is computed, from diameter, height and form. It therefore inherits every error made in those three. That is why the module measures diameter before it measures anything else.

Section 4Objectives and importance

The four objectives

  1. Basis of sale. Royalty, auction price and contract value are charged on measured volume. The measurement is the money, and an error in the formula is an error in revenue — in both directions.
  2. Basis for management. The working plan prescribes what may be felled and what must be left. Its central control, the annual allowable cut, is a volume.
  3. Research. Volume equations, form-factor functions, growth models and yield tables are all built from carefully measured trees.
  4. Planning. Investment in roads, mills and processing depends on knowing what the resource contains and how fast it is growing.

A useful ordering device: the four objectives run forward in time. Sale is now. Management is the plan period. Research is the coming decades. Planning is the projects not yet begun.

Six questions a forester must be able to answer

  1. How much wood is standing here?
  2. How fast is it growing?
  3. How much may be cut without depleting the forest?
  4. What is it worth?
  5. Has the forest improved or declined since it was last measured?
  6. How confident am I in each of those answers?

The sixth question is the professional one, and it is the one this module keeps returning to.

Section 5The accuracy attainable

“In forest mensuration, absolute accuracy is
neither necessary nor desirable.”

This sentence sounds like an excuse for sloppy work. It is not. It is a statement about what the material allows and about what accuracy costs. Learn to justify it rather than merely repeat it.

The six reasons only relative accuracy is attainable

You must be able to name and explain all six.

  1. The characteristics of trees. No tree is a true solid of revolution. Stems are elliptical, fluted, buttressed, leaning and hollow. Every formula in this module assumes a shape the tree does not actually have.
  2. Varying methods and conditions of felling and conversion. How high the stump is cut, where the stem is crosscut and what counts as merchantable all change the volume recorded from the same tree.
  3. Instruments and the conditions of their use. A clinometer read at two degrees out, a tape not perpendicular, a calliper out of square — the instrument's limits are inherited by the measurement.
  4. The biological character of the forest. A forest grows while you measure it, changes between one season and the next, and may be destroyed by fire or wind before the plan that used the figures is executed.
  5. The use to which the measurement is to be put. A reconnaissance for a thinning decision does not need the accuracy of a timber sale.
  6. Cost. Accuracy is bought with time, people and instruments, and the budget is finite. Doubling precision costs about four times as much effort.
The professional judgement

The right question is never “how accurate can I be?” but “how accurate does this particular job need to be, and what will that cost?” A forester who cannot answer that wastes either money or credibility.

Section 6Precision and accuracy

These are not synonyms, and the distinction decides whether a bad instrument can be rescued by taking more readings. It cannot.

AccurateNot accurate
Precise Tight cluster on the true value. What you want. Tight cluster in the wrong place. The dangerous case — it looks like good data.
Not precise Scattered around the true value. Noisy but unbiased; more readings will fix it. Scattered and off-centre. Obviously bad, and at least it announces itself.
The rule to carry into every unit

Random error cancels. Bias does not. Measuring twice as many trees with a stretched tape gives a more precise wrong answer. More data fixes imprecision; only calibration, correction or a change of procedure fixes bias.

Worked example · two tapes, one tree

Worked example 1

The same stem is measured five times with each of two girth tapes. The true girth is 94.0 cm.

Tape12345Mean
A96.196.096.296.096.296.1
B92.495.893.195.093.794.0

Tape A is highly precise — the readings agree to within 0.2 cm — and inaccurate: every reading is about 2 cm high. It has stretched. No amount of extra measurement will find that out, because the error is the same every time.

Tape B is imprecise — a spread of 3.4 cm — but accurate on average. Its mean lands on the true value. More readings would tighten it.

A is the dangerous tape, because its data look good

Section 7In Bhutanese forestry

Why this is a national obligation, not a technical hobby

Article 5 of the Constitution requires a minimum of sixty per cent of Bhutan's total land under forest cover for all time to come. Somebody has to measure that, and it is measured with the methods in this module.

The National Forest Policy of Bhutan 2011 frames forestry around equitable benefit, people-centred management, good science combined with indigenous knowledge, biodiversity, ecosystem services, climate change and cultural values. Read that list and notice how little of it is timber.

The Forest and Nature Conservation Act 2023 governs State Reserved Forest Land and forestry clearance — Unit III shows how a canopy figure becomes a legal decision under section 46 (2). The National Biodiversity Strategy and Action Plan 2025 reinforces conservation, sustainable use and multipurpose forest management.

Forest cover at the Second National Forest Inventory was 69.71 % (State of Forest Report 2023). Quote it with its source; it is revised at each inventory cycle.

Section 8Common errors

  1. Using precision and accuracy as if they meant the same thing.
  2. Claiming that more measurements fix any error. They fix random error only.
  3. Reciting the six reasons for relative accuracy without being able to explain any of them. The examination asks for explanation.
  4. Quoting a result to more figures than the measurement supports — writing 18.4732 m from a clinometer that is honest to about half a metre.
  5. Treating the definition loosely. It is examinable verbatim.
  6. Saying “volume is the objective of mensuration”. It is the most consequential single variable, and one of many.

Section 10Summary

  • Forest mensuration determines dimensions, form, volume, age and increment of single trees, stands or whole woods, standing or felled.
  • It deals in linear, area, volume and weight measurement — L, L², L³ and M.
  • Volume carries the most decisions and is never measured directly on a standing tree. It is one variable among fourteen the discipline is asked to supply.
  • The four objectives are sale, management, research and planning, running forward in time.
  • Only relative accuracy is attainable, for six nameable reasons — tree character, felling and conversion practice, instruments, the biology of the forest, the use of the figure, and cost.
  • Precision is agreement with each other; accuracy is agreement with reality. Random error cancels; bias does not.
  • In Bhutan the whole enterprise sits under a constitutional commitment to 60 % forest cover.
