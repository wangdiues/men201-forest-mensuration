The decisions that rest on a measured number

Why mensuration is the foundation subject

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit I · Session 2
Silviculture

A thinning, a shelterwood or a felling is prescribed as a number — a basal area to leave, a diameter limit, a stocking. The prescription is written in mensuration units.

Management planning

A working plan turns on the annual allowable cut. That is a volume, derived from inventory and growth data collected by the methods of this module.

Revenue and sale

Timber is sold by volume or weight. A measurement error is a pricing error, and it transfers money between the state and the buyer in one direction only.

Research

A trial that cannot be measured cannot be concluded. Plot layout, remeasurement and increment analysis are all mensuration procedures.

Carbon and reporting

Biomass and carbon stocks are volume estimates multiplied by density and expansion factors. National reporting inherits every error in the volume.

Policy

"Forest cover shall not fall below 60 %" is a measurement statement. The threshold means nothing until somebody defines and measures what counts.

69.71 %

Forest cover in Bhutan, as reported in the National Forest Inventory. Every national figure of this kind — cover, growing stock, carbon — begins as a field measurement taken by a forester with an instrument in their hand. A wrong number never announces itself; it simply propagates.

Section A · Where the word comes from

In Latin, mensura means measure

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit I · Session 2

In Latin, the word “mensura” means the measure of length, areas and volumes. The name of the subject therefore states its own content.

  • The same root gives English measure, dimension, commensurate and immense — the vocabulary of assigning number to extent
  • In mathematics, mensuration is the branch that computes the length, area and volume of regular geometric figures — the cylinder, cone, frustum and sphere
  • Forest mensuration takes that same geometry and applies it to trees, which are not regular geometric figures
  • Forestry adds a fourth quantity the Latin does not name: weight, used for fuelwood, pulpwood, foliage and biomass
The tension to keep in mind all session

We use the mathematics of perfect solids on an object that is never a perfect solid. Everything in Section C follows from that single mismatch.

The mathematics we bring · the object we are given

Section A · The definition — copy this exactly

Forest mensuration, formally defined

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit I · Session 2
Definition

“Forest mensuration is that branch of forestry which deals with the determination of dimensions (e.g. diameter, height, volume etc.), form, age and increment of a single tree, stands or whole woods, either standing or after felling.”

What is determined

Dimensions, form, age and increment. Not one of the four is optional — a deck of diameters with no age and no increment describes a forest that cannot be managed through time.

On what

A single tree, a stand, or whole woods. The subject scales from one stem measured with a tape to a national inventory covering a country.

In what state

Standing or after felling. The standing tree must be measured indirectly and estimated; the felled tree can be measured directly and checked.

Therefore

Forest mensuration concerns itself with linear, area, volume and weight measurement — and of these, volume is the most important measurement for forest management.

Where the units go wrong

A tree carries cubic metres. A stand carries cubic metres per hectare. Dropping or duplicating the “per hectare” at one step is the commonest calculation error in this module — and it changes the answer by a factor of the plot size.

Because a stand figure comes from a sample, it carries a sampling error as well as a measurement error. Section C deals with the second; sampling deals with the first, later in the module.

Section A · The quantity everything else serves

Volume is the most important measurement

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit I · Session 2

Volume is the currency of forest management: it is what is sold, what is prescribed, what is grown and what is reported.

  • Timber is traded in cubic metres — the sale price is a volume multiplied by a rate
  • The annual allowable cut, the central control of sustained yield, is a volume
  • Growing stock and yield tables are volume statements of what a forest holds and will hold
  • Increment — the growth a forest can afford to give up — is volume per hectare per year
  • Biomass and carbon figures begin as volume, then are multiplied by density and expansion factors
And yet

Volume on a standing tree is never measured — it is computed, from diameter, height and form. It therefore inherits every error made in those three.

V = g · h · f volume = basal area × height × form factor  ·  and basal area g = πd²/4
Error in d → error in V, roughly doubled

Section B · Objective 1

Basis of sale — the measurement is the money

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit I · Session 2

Forest produce is sold by quantity. Estimate the volume, apply a rate set by supply and demand, and the sale value follows.

  • Standing timber is marked and enumerated, and its volume estimated before any tree is felled
  • The estimate sets the upset price for auction, or the rate for a negotiated sale
  • After felling, the out-turn is measured again — and the two figures are compared
  • Non-timber produce is measured too: fuelwood by stacked volume or weight, poles by number and size, bamboo and resin by piece or weight
Why volume, not weight, for timber

Green weight and dry weight differ by a large margin, and the weight of a stack changes from week to week. Volume does not change with moisture. Where weight is used, the moisture basis must be stated.

The chain from tree to payment
1 · Mark
select and mark the trees
2 · Enumerate
record d, h, species
3 · Estimate
V = g · h · f or volume table
4 · Value
V × rate = sale price
5 · Check
measure the out-turn, compare
There is no neutral error

An overestimate makes the buyer overpay; an underestimate gives away public timber. In a sale, measurement rules are agreed in advance and written down, because both parties have money resting on them.

Section B · Objectives 3 and 4

Measurement for research and for planning

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit I · Session 2
3
Measurement for research

Data collection and research plot layout — the evidence from which forestry knowledge is built.

  • Trials must be laid out before they can be measured: plot size, shape, replication, buffer
  • Growth and yield models, volume tables and site indices are all products of measured plots
  • Long-term questions need consistent measurement over decades — the protocol matters as much as the instrument
  • A result is only as trustworthy as the measurement rule behind it

If two plots are measured by two people using two conventions, the difference between treatments cannot be separated from the difference between observers.

4
Measurement for planning

Statistical data to plan for the future, and a yardstick to check the viability of projects.

  • Projections of yield, revenue and cost rest on measured growth rates
  • Plantation programmes, road alignments and processing units are justified by numbers produced here
  • Measurement is also the yardstick after the fact — did the project deliver what was projected?
  • National statistics on cover, growing stock and carbon are planning data at the largest scale

Without the check afterwards, no project is ever proved wrong — and a project that is never proved wrong will be repeated.

What these two share

Both look forward rather than at the present, and both depend on measurement that stays consistent over long periods. Changing an instrument or a convention half way through destroys a time series as effectively as not measuring at all.

Section C · The starting position

Forest mensuration is not mathematics

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit I · Session 2
The comparison
A machined part
  • Made to a specified shape
  • Measured to a hundredth of a millimetre
  • Gives the same answer tomorrow
  • The true value exists and can be checked
error ≈ 0.01 %
The reality
A standing tree
  • Made to no specification at all
  • Measured through leaves, on a slope, in the rain
  • Grows between one visit and the next
  • The true value is never known, so error cannot be checked
error of several per cent is normal
The consequence
What honesty requires
  • Report a figure with an indication of how good it is
  • Never quote more decimal places than the instrument can support
  • Record the method, the instrument and the observer
  • Decide the accuracy needed before going to the field
a number plus its provenance
Say it precisely

“Forest mensuration is not as accurate as mathematical calculations” is a statement about the object, not about the forester. The mathematics is exact. The tree is not. A volume quoted to four decimal places from a tape read to the nearest centimetre is not more accurate — it is dishonest.

Section C · The claim that provokes an argument

“Accuracy is neither necessary nor desirable”

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit I · Session 2
Not necessary

Because the decision the measurement feeds is coarse. Whether a compartment is thinned or not thinned is decided just as well by a growing stock estimate good to 10 % as by one good to 1 %.

A measurement that changes no decision has bought nothing.

Not desirable

Because accuracy is bought with time and money from a finite budget. Money spent measuring one compartment to 1 % is money not spent measuring nine other compartments at all.

Excess accuracy in one place produces ignorance in another.

Accuracy only matters where it can move the answer across a boundary
Guard against the wrong conclusion

This is not permission to be careless. Carelessness produces bias, and bias is not made acceptable by a tolerant view of accuracy. The correct reading is: decide what accuracy the job needs, then achieve that level properly.

Section C · Reason 1 of 6

Characteristics of trees — no tree is a solid of revolution

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit I · Session 2

No tree shows a geometrical form. On the whole earth, each tree has its own set of forms, shapes and sizes.

  • Every volume formula we possess assumes a cylinder, cone, frustum or paraboloid — and no stem is any of them
  • The formula is exact; the assumption behind it is false for every tree to which it is applied
  • Cross-sections are not circles, so a girth tape — which converts girth to diameter assuming a circle — systematically overestimates diameter
  • That is a bias: always in the same direction, and not removed by taking more readings
The residual is not your mistake

It is the gap between a real stem and the ideal solid we substituted for it in order to be able to compute anything at all.

Buttress · sweep · fork · flute · out-of-round

Section C · Reason 2 of 6

Varying methods and conditions of felling and conversion

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit I · Session 2

Skillfully felled and converted timber will produce more volume than the work of unskilled labour — from the same tree.

  • Stump height — a stump left at 50 cm instead of 10 cm discards 40 cm of the largest-diameter part of the stem
  • Cross-cutting — where the stem is cut decides how many usable lengths come out of it and how much becomes waste
  • Breakage — a tree dropped across a rock or into a gully splits and is downgraded; one felled into a prepared lay is not
  • Conditions — slope, weather, tools and the skill of the crew all change the out-turn
The mensuration consequence

The volume that leaves the forest is not a property of the tree alone — it is a property of the tree, the crew and the day. So a difference between the standing estimate and the out-turn is not automatically a measurement error.

Section C · Reason 3 of 6

Instruments — and the conditions of their use

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit I · Session 2
The instrument itself
  • Different principles: a tape measures girth, a calliper measures a chord, a laser measures a distance and an angle
  • Different assumptions: a circular section, a visible tree top, level ground, a vertical stem — each false somewhere
  • Different resolutions: to the nearest centimetre, half metre or metre
  • Each has an error characteristic of its design, which training cannot remove
The way it is used
  • Standing too close to the tree, or measuring from up- or downhill without correction
  • Sighting a side branch as the top of a broadleaved crown
  • A tape held slack, twisted, or not perpendicular to the stem axis
  • Reading a calliper once instead of averaging two perpendicular readings
  • These are operator errors — and unlike the first column, they are correctable

Section C · Reason 4 of 6

The biological character of the forest

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit I · Session 2

A forest is a living entity. It is affected by many other factors — especially locality factors — and those factors cannot be predicted accurately by calculation.

It changes while you measure it

A measurement is a snapshot of something that grows during the field season, that will be different next year, and that may be lost to windthrow, fire, pest or landslide before the plan period ends.

Its drivers vary over metres

Soil depth, aspect, moisture, altitude, exposure and the weather of a particular decade control growth — and they change across a single hillside. Two plots 100 m apart can differ in site quality more than two plots in different districts.

The consequence for measurement

Variation between trees and between plots is real, not error. If ten plots give ten different figures, that spread is mostly the forest, not the forester. Sampling exists to quantify that spread — not to remove it.

Locality factors change over metres of ground

The principle

Accuracy is matched to the value of the decision, not to the capability of the instrument. Measuring fuelwood roughly is professional — provided the roughness is deliberate, stated and appropriate. It is unprofessional only when it is accidental or concealed.

Section C · Reason 6 of 6

Cost — accuracy is bought, and the budget is finite

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit I · Session 2
Where the two curves cross is the professional target

More accuracy means more cost. Accuracy is therefore based on economic viability — not on what the instrument is capable of.

error ∝ 1 / √n to halve the sampling error, roughly quadruple the number of plots
  • Going from a rough guess to a decent estimate is cheap
  • Going from a decent estimate to a very good one is expensive
  • Going from very good to nearly perfect is close to unaffordable
  • Meanwhile the value of the extra accuracy flattens, because beyond a point it changes no decision

Section D · The two definitions — copy these exactly

Precision and accuracy are not synonyms

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit I · Session 2
Definition
Precision

The degree of agreement in a series of measurements.

  • A property of a series, not of one reading — a single measurement has no precision
  • Measured by the spread: range, standard deviation, coefficient of variation
  • Improved by better technique, a finer instrument, and more readings
Definition
Accuracy

The closeness of a measurement to the true value.

  • Requires a true value — which for a standing tree is never known
  • Usually inferred by checking against a better method, or from the design of the instrument
  • Improved by calibration and correction — never by more readings
One sentence to remember the whole section by

Precision is the measurements agreeing with each other. Accuracy is the measurements agreeing with reality.

And the consequence

Repeating a measurement improves precision and does nothing whatever about bias. A tape that reads two centimetres high reads two centimetres high a thousand times — and the mean of a thousand readings is exactly two centimetres wrong.

Section D · The standard illustration

Four ways a set of measurements can turn out

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration

The centre of the target is the true value
Read the diagram this way
  • The centre is the true value; each dot is one measurement
  • Precision is how tightly the dots cluster with each other
  • Accuracy is how close the cluster sits to the centre
  • The two are independent — every combination of high and low can occur
Why the top-right case is the dangerous one

The readings agree beautifully. The field sheet looks excellent. Every figure is wrong by the same amount — and nothing inside the data reveals it. Only a check against an independent standard does.

This is what calibration is for

Field protocols require instruments to be checked against a known standard before the season, precisely because bias is invisible from inside the data set it contaminates.

  • Cannot be detected from inside the data — only against an external standard

A tape stretched by years of use · measuring above a buttress because the buttress is in the way · always sighting from downhill · a girth tape on out-of-round stems

Curecalibrate and correct
The examinable sentence

Increasing the number of measurements reduces random error and leaves bias untouched. If you remember one thing from Section D, remember that — it is why a precise instrument can be worse than an imprecise one.

A tree is felled and its diameter at breast height is found to be exactly 42.0 cm. Before felling, two students each measured it five times, with different tapes.

Not precise — the readings spread over 2.3 cm.

Carry the error through to volume
True basal area
g = π(0.420)²/4 = 0.1385 m²
From A's mean
g = π(0.4412)²/4 = 0.1529 m²
Error in d
+2.12 / 42.0 = +5.0 %
Error in g and V
0.1529 / 0.1385 − 1 = +10.4 %
a 5 % bias in d → a 10 % bias in V because volume follows the square of diameter
Now scale it up

Apply Student A's tape to a compartment of a thousand trees and the growing stock is overstated by about 10 %. That figure goes into the working plan, sets the annual allowable cut, and the compartment is overcut — and nothing on the field sheets would ever have revealed it.

Session summary

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit I · Session 2
A
Defined
  • Latin mensura — measure of length, area and volume
  • The branch of forestry dealing with dimensions, form, age and increment
  • Of a single tree, stands or whole woods
  • Either standing or after felling
  • Concerns linear, area, volume and weight
  • Volume is the most important measurement for management
  • Volume is computed, not measured: V = g·h·f
B
Objectives
  • Basis of sale — volume and price from supply and demand
  • Basis for management — inventory data for the plan and its implementation
  • For research — data collection and plot layout
  • For planning — statistics for the future and a yardstick for viability
  • Importance is best shown by the six questions only measurement can answer
  • The accuracy each question needs is not the same
C
Accuracy
  • Forest mensuration is not as accurate as mathematics
  • Absolute accuracy is neither necessary nor desirable
  • 1 · Characteristics of trees — no geometrical form
  • 2 · Felling and conversion — skill changes the out-turn
  • 3 · Instruments and the conditions of their use
  • 4 · Biological character — locality factors
  • 5 · The use the measurement is put to
  • 6 · Cost — accuracy rests on economic viability
D
Precision & accuracy
  • Precision — degree of agreement in a series of measurements
  • Accuracy — closeness of a measurement to the true value
  • Precision is agreement with each other; accuracy is agreement with reality
  • The two are independent — all four combinations occur
  • Random error cancels; bias does not
  • More readings fix precision, never bias
  • A 5 % bias in d becomes a 10 % bias in V

A figure without its method and its accuracy is not a measurement — it is a claim.
