Section 1Learning outcomes

  1. Define total height and distinguish the six heights measured along a tree.
  2. Estimate height without instruments by the ocular, shadow and single-pole methods, and state the assumptions each makes.
  3. Apply the tangent method in all four standing positions, adding eye height correctly.
  4. Recognise when a tree leans enough to invalidate the tangent method, and apply the sine method in all six cases.
  5. Identify the seven sources of error and say which are random and which systematic.
  6. Assign trees to DBH and height classes using the standard width rules.

Section 2Which height?

“Height” on its own is not a measurement. Six different heights are read from one tree, and they answer different questions.

#HeightFrom → toWho wants it
1Total heightGround level → tip of the leading shootVolume tables, site quality, growth studies
2Bole heightGround → crown point, the first crown-forming branch living or deadSilviculturist — describes how the stem is carried
3Commercial bole heightGround → the last point fit for utilisationBuyer, sawmiller — how many logs
4Stump heightGround → the top of the stump after fellingHarvesting supervisor — the most controllable loss on a coupe
5Crown lengthTip → base of the live crownResearcher — live-crown ratio, vigour, competition
6Crown-base heightGround → base of the live crownResearcher, fire officer — the ladder-fuel gap
crown length + crown-base height = total height  ·  live-crown ratio = crown length ÷ total height
Two that look the same and are not

Bole height stops at the first crown-forming branch, living or dead. Crown-base height stops at the base of the live crown. On a tree that has kept its dead branches, bole height is the shorter of the two.

The bare term “crown height” is not used in this module — see Unit III for why.

Why height is the hardest dimension

Volume is linear in height. A 10 % error in the height of a tree is a 10 % error in its volume — there is no averaging, no cancellation, and no way to recover it later in the office. Diameter you can walk up to and touch; height you must infer from an angle and a distance, and both can be wrong.

Section 3Measuring without instruments

Ocular estimation

Judging height by eye. Fast, free, and genuinely good with practice against known trees. Systematically poor without calibration, and worse with tall trees than short ones. Use it as a check on an instrument reading, never as the reading.

The shadow method

Similar triangles. Measure a pole, the pole's shadow, and the tree's shadow.

tree height = pole height × (tree shadow ÷ pole shadow)
Worked example 1 · shadow method

Pole 2.0 m, pole shadow 1.6 m, tree shadow 14.4 m.

h = 2.0 × 14.4 ÷ 1.6 = 18.0 m

The sensitivity to watch. Suppose the pole shadow were measured five minutes later and read 1.7 m instead of 1.6 m: h = 2.0 × 14.4 ÷ 1.7 = 16.9 m. A 6 cm error in one small measurement has moved the tree by more than a metre. Measure all three within the same few minutes, because the sun moves — and beware any ratio whose denominator is small.

Assumptions: level ground, an unobstructed shadow, and the sun out. It cannot be used inside a closed broadleaf stand at midday, because no shadow reaches the floor to measure.

The single pole method

Hold a pole of known length vertically at arm's length, move until the pole visually covers the tree exactly, then use similar triangles between eye–pole and eye–tree. Cheap and surprisingly good; sensitive to holding the pole truly vertical and to a consistent arm's length.

Section 4The tangent method

The professional standard where the tree is vertical. An instrument reads an angle; you supply the distance and do the arithmetic.

Instruments

InstrumentPrinciple
Christen's hypsometerGeometric — similar triangles, no distance needed, but a pole of known length must be held at the tree
Smythies' hypsometerGeometric — a fixed base distance is set out
ClinometerTrigonometric — reads the angle; you supply the distance
Laser hypsometerTrigonometric — measures the distance itself and does the arithmetic internally
Which instrument, in which setting

The clinometer is cheap, rugged, needs no batteries, and the crew already carries one for slope correction — which is why it dominates teaching exercises and resource-limited field work. Bhutan's Second National Forest Inventory used a Haglöf Laser Geo hypsometer and measured every tree in the plot. Name the setting before you name the instrument.

The four cases

a · level ground
h = D · tan θ + eye height D = horizontal distance · θ = elevation to the tip
b · eye between top and base
AB = EB · cos β (tan α + tan β) α elevation to the top · β depression to the base · EB slope distance to the base
c · eye below the base
AB = EB · cos β (tan α − tan β) both angles are elevations — the whole tree is above your horizontal
d · eye above the base
AB = EB · cos β (tan β − tan α) both angles are depressions — β to the base is the steeper
Worked example 2 · level ground

Horizontal distance D = 20.0 m, elevation to tip θ = 38°, eye height 1.5 m.

tan 38°0.7813
Height above the eye20.0 × 0.7813 = 15.63 m
Add eye height15.63 + 1.50 = 17.13 m
h = 17.1 m

15.63 m is the height above the eye — never the height of the tree. Adding the eye height back is the step students omit.

How much does 2° cost? Read as 40° instead of 38°: 20.0 × 0.8391 + 1.5 = 18.3 m. A two-degree misreading has moved the tree by 1.2 m, about 7 %. Read the instrument twice and average.

Worked example 3 · sloping ground, eye between top and base

Slope distance EB = 18.0 m, depression to base β = 12°, elevation to top α = 34°.

Horizontal distanceED = 18.0 × cos 12° = 17.61 m
Tangentstan 34° = 0.6745 · tan 12° = 0.2126
Sum0.6745 + 0.2126 = 0.8871
Height17.61 × 0.8871 = 15.62 m
h = 15.6 m

Sanity-check it physically. Tip above eye 17.61 × 0.6745 = 11.88 m; base below eye 17.61 × 0.2126 = 3.74 m; total 15.62 m ✓. If you cannot explain the answer in those two physical parts, you have not understood it.

The commonest error on a slope

Measuring with the eye between top and base and then adding the eye height at the end. That double-counts: the depression angle β to the base has already measured the drop from eye to base. Eye height is added only in the level-ground case.

Section 5Leaning trees and the sine method

Every tangent formula assumes the tree is vertical — that the tip stands directly above the base. A leaning tree breaks that assumption, and the tangent method then measures an imaginary vertical tree through the tip.

Which way the error goes

A tree leaning towards you reads too tall: the imaginary vertical tree through the tip is taller than the real stem. Leaning away reads too short.

The master equation

The sine rule on triangle AEB, where A is the tip, B the base and E the eye:

AB / sin ∠AEB = BE / sin ∠EAB = AE / sin ∠ABE
AB = EB · sin ∠AEB ÷ sin ∠EAB the master equation — the six cases below only find ∠EAB
Why foresters like it

EB is a slope distance, read straight along the line of sight — and it stays one. The sine method never needs the horizontal distance, which is its whole advantage on steep ground.

The six cases

Three positions × two lean directions. θ is the angle of lean.

CaseWhere you standAngles readLeaning towards youLeaning away
aBetween the base and the topα elevation, β depression EB · sin(α+β) / cos(α−θ)EB · sin(α+β) / cos(α+θ)
bBelow the base and the topboth elevations EB · sin(α−β) / cos(α−θ)EB · sin(α−β) / cos(α+θ)
cAbove the base and the topboth depressions EB · sin(β−α) / cos(α+θ)EB · sin(β−α) / cos(α−θ)
How to use the table — two questions, never six formulae

Where you stand chooses the row, and therefore the numerator. Which way it leans chooses the column, and therefore the sign inside the cosine. Three numerators × two signs = six cases. Do not memorise six formulae; memorise two questions.

The physical check: a bigger denominator gives a smaller height, which matches the fact that a tree leaning away is further off than it looks. Full derivations of the denominators are in the calculation handbook.

Section 6Sources of error

SourceNatureCure
InstrumentalSystematicCalibrate; check the zero
Personal — one observer always sights highSystematicTwo observers on a subsample, compared
Observational — misreading the scaleRandomRead twice and average
Weather — wind moving the crownRandomRepeat; avoid gusty conditions
Wrong tip identified in a dense crownSystematic (usually low)Move until the true leader is visible
Slope distance used as horizontal distanceSystematic (high)Use the correct case formula
Leaning stem measured by tangentSystematic (either way)Use the sine method
The distinction that decides the cure

Instrumental and personal errors are systematic and therefore never average out — only better procedure removes them. Observational and weather errors are largely random and do average out with repetition. That distinction decides whether the answer is more measurements or better procedure.

Six habits that remove most error

  1. Stand at roughly one tree length from the tree — closer exaggerates angle error, further makes the tip hard to see.
  2. Make sure you can see both the tip and the base from where you stand.
  3. Measure the distance, do not pace it, on anything that matters.
  4. Read the instrument twice and average.
  5. Check whether the stem leans before choosing a formula.
  6. Round once, at the end — not at every step.

Section 7DBH and height classes

For research, every tree's dimensions are recorded separately. For routine measurement they are not needed: trees are allotted to a class, and the number in each class is recorded.

If the height is…Use a class width ofExample intervals
under 15 m1 m10–11 · 11–12 · 12–13 …
15 to 25 m3 m15–18 · 18–21 · 21–24 …
over 25 m5 m25–30 · 30–35 · 35–40 …
The one students get wrong

The 3 m height series starts at 15 m, not at zero — 15–18, 18–21, 21–24 — because 15 m is where the rule switches on. Counting from zero puts 19.6 m in the wrong row.

The principle behind both rules: the class width follows the density of the data. Small trees are numerous and tightly packed, so a narrow class is needed to show structure; large trees are few and widely spread, so a narrow class would give rows holding one stem each. The aim is a roughly comparable number of trees in each class.

Why height classes broaden faster than diameter classes

Not only because tall trees are fewer — because tall trees are measured less accurately. A 30 m tree cannot be measured to the nearest metre with a clinometer in a real forest, so a 1 m class would be recording precision that does not exist. A class width should never be finer than the measurement error.

Measured, or modelled?

Bhutan's Second NFI measured the height of every tree in the plot with a laser hypsometer. Many FMU, community-forest and teaching inventories measure a subsample and fill in the rest from a height–diameter curve. In that case the height class summarises a modelled quantity — and you say so when you report it. Do not present subsampling as a national convention; it is a practice, adopted or not according to the manual governing the job.

Section 8In Bhutanese forestry

  • Growing-stock estimation. V = G × h × f. No height, no volume; no volume, no annual allowable cut. That is the direct line from a clinometer reading to a management decision.
  • Dominant height and site quality. The height of the dominant trees at a given age is the standard index of what a site can produce — and it is almost independent of stocking, which is exactly what makes it useful.
  • Biomass and carbon models. Bhutan's 2018 allometric equations have a DBH-only form and a DBH-and-height form. The height-inclusive one is better: Unit VI shows two trees of identical DBH differing by 37 % in biomass because their heights differ.
  • Beyond timber. Canopy structure and layering, ladder fuels and fire risk, large habitat trees, riparian shading and stream temperature. None of these is timber; all of them need height.
Community forestry

A group measures heights on a subsample to build its own height–diameter curve, and uses it to estimate standing volume for the management plan. It is the cheapest way for a group to know what it holds — and the curve is theirs to keep and re-use.

Section 9Practice questions

  1. A tree has dead branches low on the stem and green branches only near the top. Which is greater — bole height or crown-base height? Explain.
  2. Why can the shadow method not be used inside a closed broadleaf stand at midday?
  3. You measure on a slope with the eye between top and base and add 1.5 m eye height at the end. What have you done wrong?
  4. Horizontal distance 25.0 m, elevation to tip 41°, eye height 1.6 m. Compute the total height. Then recompute with the angle read as 43° and state the error in metres and per cent.
  5. Slope distance 22.0 m, elevation to top 29°, depression to base 9°. Compute the height, and check it in two physical parts.
  6. You stand on a ridge; the tree is below you and leans away. Which formula applies?
  7. A tree leans towards you and you use the tangent method. Is your answer too large or too small? Why?
  8. A tree measures exactly 50.0 cm DBH and 24.8 m tall. State both classes.
  9. Explain why height classes broaden with height faster than diameter classes broaden with diameter.
  10. An inventory reports a mean height of 21.7 m for a stand in which only twelve of ninety trees were measured. What must the report also say, and why?

Worked solutions to questions 4, 5 and 8 are in the calculation handbook.

Section 10Summary

  • Name the height before you measure it. Six heights, six purposes.
  • Volume is linear in height — a 10 % height error is a 10 % volume error, unrecoverable.
  • Tangent method: h = D tan θ + eye height on level ground; AB = EB cos β (tan α ± tan β) on a slope. Add eye height only in the level-ground case.
  • Sine method for leaning stems: AB = EB · sin∠AEB / sin∠EAB. Position picks the numerator; lean direction picks the sign in the cosine.
  • Systematic errors never average out. Random ones do.
  • Class width follows data density, and is never finer than the measurement error.
