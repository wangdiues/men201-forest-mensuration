Section 1Learning outcomes

  1. Describe stem form using the four solids of revolution, and say where each appears in a real stem.
  2. Calculate and interpret a form factor, a form height and a form quotient.
  3. Apply Smalian's, Huber's and Newton's formulae, and state which is exact for what.
  4. Convert between true volume and quarter-girth volume and quantify the loss.
  5. Read and interpolate a volume table, and classify tables by variables, locality and material.
  6. Apply the Department's Model 16 for Pinus wallichiana, and state its domain and limitations.
  7. Separate definitional difference, model error and measurement error when two volume figures disagree.

Section 2Stem form

A stem is not a cylinder. Its cross-sectional area falls with distance from the tip according to a single exponent r:

S = k · xr S = cross-sectional area · x = distance from the tip · r fixes the shape
SolidrTaperWhere it appears in a stem
Cylinder0noneNo real stem — the reference shape for the form factor
Paraboloid1slightThe middle of most stems
Cone2steadyThe top, above the crown base
Neiloid3concave, flaringThe butt swell at the base

Metzger's girder theory explains why: a stem is loaded by wind on the crown and behaves like a cantilever beam of uniform resistance to bending. Such a beam is a cubic paraboloid. The practical consequence is that a stem exposed to more wind — an isolated tree, or one on a ridge — is more heavily tapered, and a stem grown in a dense stand is more cylindrical.

Why cutting into short lengths matters

Smalian's, Huber's and Newton's formulae are all exact for a paraboloid frustum and approximate for anything else. Over a 4 m length a real stem is close to a paraboloid frustum. Over 25 m it passes through neiloid, paraboloid and cone, and no single formula holds. Cutting into short sections keeps each piece inside the range where the geometry is honest.

Section 3Form factor and form quotient

Form factor

f = V ÷ (S × h) V tree volume, m³ · S cross-sectional area at the point of measurement, m² · h height, m
f is dimensionless

Reading the number. f = 1 is a perfect cylinder. f = 0.50 means the tree holds half the wood of the cylinder that encloses it. Real stems mostly fall between about 0.35 and 0.55.

Three classes of form factor

ClassWhere S is measuredNote
ArtificialAt breast heightThe common one. Called artificial precisely because breast height is an arbitrary human convenience, not a feature of the tree
AbsoluteAt any convenient point, usually the baseRemoves the arbitrariness but is harder to standardise
NormalAt a constant proportion of total height — 1/10, 1/20 …Comparable between trees of different size
Worked example 1 · calculating a form factor

A blue pine is felled. Its stem volume, from measuring every 2 m section, is 1.35 m³. Before felling its DBH was 40 cm and total height 22 m.

Square the diameter40² = 1 600 cm²
Basal areaS = 0.00007854 × 1 600 = 0.125664 m²
Cylinder volume0.125664 × 22 = 2.764608 m³
Form factorf = 1.35 ÷ 2.764608 = 0.4883
f = 0.488 — this stem holds 49 % of its enclosing cylinder

Form height

form height = f × h  →  V = S × (form height)

A convenience: it collapses two of the three variables into one, so a volume table can be indexed on diameter and form height alone.

Form quotient

Schiffel's idea: you do not need the whole profile of a stem — one well-chosen upper diameter tells you most of what you need.

FQ = mid-diameter ÷ DBH
The problem with the simple form quotient

For a tree whose total height is twice breast height — about 2.6 m — half the total height falls at breast height itself, so the mid-diameter is the DBH and FQ = 1 whatever the tree's real shape. For anything shorter the mid-point falls below breast height and there is no mid-diameter to read at all. The ratio stops discriminating.

AFQ = diameter at half the height above breast height ÷ DBH

That is the absolute form quotient, and it discriminates properly at all tree sizes.

Worked example 2 · form quotient and absolute form quotient

A hemlock has DBH 40 cm and total height 24 m. Upper-stem diameters read with a relascope: 26 cm at 12.0 m and 25 cm at 12.65 m.

Simple FQmid-point 24/2 = 12.0 m → 26/40 = 0.650
AFQ point1.3 + (24 − 1.3)/2 = 1.3 + 11.35 = 12.65 m
AFQ25/40 = 0.625

Subtract breast height first, then halve, then add it back. Students who halve the total height and then add 1.3 get 13.3 m instead of 12.65 m.

The AFQ is always the lower figure for the same tree, because its measurement point is higher and a stem is always narrower higher up. An AFQ of 0.625 says the stem still holds 62.5 % of its breast-height diameter at the midpoint of its usable length — a reasonably cylindrical stem. Below about 0.55 indicates heavy taper.

FQ and f are not interchangeable

They are related but not equal. Schiffel showed volume can be predicted from FQ, which is a different claim from saying FQ equals f.

Conifers cluster around 0.48–0.55; broadleaves fall away, and the two lowest are oaks — because oaks fork low and put mass into large branches, so a single-stem cylinder is a poor container for them. Never apply a conifer form factor to a broadleaf; the error would be 40 % or more.

Two qualifications on any published form factor

First, these are means. Individual trees vary, and the study reported residual standard deviations of 8–16 % of observed values. Applying a species mean to a single tree carries that uncertainty.

Second, the study measured DBH at 1.37 m. DBH reference height is protocol-dependent — a published constant always carries the conventions of the study that produced it, and those conventions must be read before the number is used.

Worked example 3 · standing-tree volume from a published form factor

A fir in an FMU at 3 200 m has DBH 55 cm and total height 28 m. No local volume table is available. Use f = 0.493.

Basal areaS = 0.00007854 × 55² = 0.237583 m²
Cylinder0.237583 × 28 = 6.652 m³
Volume6.652 × 0.493 = 3.279 m³
V ≈ 3.28 m³

Reality check: the enclosing cylinder holds 6.652 m³ and roughly half of it is wood. That is what a form factor of about 0.5 means.

Worked example 4 · one log, three formulae

A 4.00 m spruce log, over bark: thick end 46 cm, mid-point 40 cm, thin end 34 cm.

S₁0.00007854 × 46² = 0.166191 m²
Sm0.00007854 × 40² = 0.125664 m²
S₂0.00007854 × 34² = 0.090792 m²
Smalian[(0.166191 + 0.090792)/2] × 4.00 = 0.513965 m³  (+1.49 %)
Huber0.125664 × 4.00 = 0.502656 m³  (−0.75 %)
Newton[(0.166191 + 4(0.125664) + 0.090792)/6] × 4.00 = 0.506426 m³
Smalian > Newton > Huber — always, on a normally tapering log

Note that Huber's error is about half Smalian's, and of opposite sign. That is not a coincidence; it falls out of the geometry.

Sectional measurement

For a whole felled stem: crosscut or mark out sections of 2 m (or 4 m for commercial work), scale each by Huber's formula, and sum. The terminal section above the last full length is scaled as a cone: v = S l / 3.

Huber is preferred per section because on a 2 m section the mid-diameter is easily reached, it needs only one reading, and it is both faster and more accurate than Smalian.

Section 5The quarter-girth formula

The old commercial convention — Hoppus measure. It replaces π with 4, which makes the arithmetic doable without a calculator, at the cost of a known systematic under-measurement.

true volume V = C² l / 4π   →   QGV = C² l / 16 = (C/4)² × l
QGV / V = π / 4 = 0.7854 the quarter-girth volume is 78.5 % of the true volume — a systematic 21.5 % under-measurement
Worked example 5 · true volume against quarter-girth volume

A chir pine log 4.00 m long; mid-point girth over bark 125 cm.

ConvertC = 1.25 m
True volume1.25² / (4π) × 4.00 = 1.5625 / 12.566371 × 4.00 = 0.4974 m³
Quarter girth1.25 / 4 = 0.3125 m
QGV0.3125² × 4.00 = 0.097656 × 4.00 = 0.3906 m³
Ratio0.3906 / 0.4974 = 0.785 ✓
The buyer gains 21.5 % of the wood, by convention
Why it survived so long

Because both parties knew about it. A systematic convention that everybody understands and prices around is not fraud — it is a unit. It becomes a problem only when one party does not know, or when a quarter-girth figure is compared with a true-volume figure without conversion.

Section 6Bark, firewood and stacked volume

Bark volume

Bark volume = volume over bark − volume under bark. Compute each from its own diameter; do not try to subtract a bark thickness from a volume.

Stacked volume

Firewood and small timber are traded stacked, and a stack is mostly air.

solid volume = stacked volume × stacking factor

A stacking factor of about 0.65 is a general working figure for neatly stacked round firewood; it falls with crooked, branchy or unsplit material. It is a general international range, not a Bhutanese constant — a factor used in an actual transaction comes from DoFPS or from local practice.

Worked example 6 · stack to solid

7.20 stacked m³ at a stacking factor of 0.65.

7.20 × 0.65 = 4.68 solid m³

Direct methods for small pieces

  • Xylometric — water displacement. Submerge the piece; the volume of water displaced is the solid volume. A level rising from 2 400 cm³ to 3 150 cm³ gives 750 cm³ = 0.00075 m³.
  • Specific gravity — weigh it and divide by the density. Requires a reliable density figure for that species at that moisture content.

Section 7Standing-tree volume and volume tables

Four routes to the volume of a standing tree:

  1. Form factor — V = S · h · f.
  2. Volume table — read it off by dimension class.
  3. Volume equation — the same relationship written as a function.
  4. Upper-stem measurement — relascope or climbing, then sectional volume. Accurate, slow, rare.

The volume table

A volume table shows, for a given species, the average contents of trees, logs or sawn timber for one or more given dimensions. Tables are built from actual measurements of a large number of representative trees.

The assumption underneath, and its consequence

That trees of the same species with the same dimensions hold the same volume. This holds on average and fails for any individual tree. A volume table gives you the average, not the volume of the tree in front of you. Use it for stands; be cautious with it for a single valuable stem.

Classification

By number of variables:

  • Single variable — usually diameter alone. Usable only in a small locality, so it is called a local volume table. Quick, because only DBH is measured.
  • Two variables — diameter and height. Applicable to larger areas, so it is called a general volume table. Local tables are usually derived from it.
  • Three variables — diameter, height and form or taper. The most accurate and the most expensive in field time.

By locality — general or local. By material — for standing trees, for felled logs, or for sawn timber out-turn.

Section 8The Bhutan volume equations

Why this equation had to be built

The pre-investment survey equations of 1974–81 predict total tree volume. When the Department changed rural timber allotment for house construction from a number of trees to a volume of logs, a total-volume equation could no longer answer the question being asked. The measurement followed the policy, not the other way round.

The merchantability standard — quote it with every figure

  • Trees of 10 cm DBH and above.
  • Sections up to a 10 cm top diameter over bark; the tip above that is discarded.
  • Branches and foliage excluded — this is log volume, not tree volume.

The two selected models

Sixteen candidate models were fitted; two were selected on AIC and BIC. Both use a restricted cubic spline term g( ), which lets the line bend without being told in advance what shape to take. The knots are the 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles of the predictor.

Model 7 — diameter alone

Model 16 — height included, and the better of the two

Is 6.1 % acceptable? It depends on a question one tree cannot answer

For a single rural timber allotment, 0.13 m³ on one stem — probably yes. For a stand estimate it depends on whether the error is random or systematic. If the model is unbiased and this tree merely sits above the line, over- and under-estimates partly offset across many trees. If the model runs high for blue pine in this region, every tree is high and so is the stand total — summation does not repair a bias. One tree cannot tell you which case you are in.

The honest weakness — say it if you use the model

Eighty-six per cent of the fitting trees came from the western valleys. A blue pine in Bumthang or Trashigang may carry a different form for the same DBH and height. The equation will still return a number, and nothing in the arithmetic will warn you.

Likewise a 140 cm stem is outside the fitted range: beyond its outer knot a cubic spline is unconstrained and can run away. Interpolate; do not extrapolate.

Three quantities students conflate

  • Total tree volume — the whole stem to the tip, sometimes branches too.
  • Merchantable stem volume — Model 16: to a 10 cm top, no branches.
  • Biomass — oven-dry mass, a different quantity in different units, from Unit VI.

A difference between two of these is not error. It is a difference of definition. Before comparing two volume figures, ask what each of them includes.

Section 9Sources of error

  • Instrument — cured by calibration.
  • Observer — cured by training and protocol.
  • Subject (exceptional trees: hollow, forked, buttressed) — cured by rules for exceptions.
  • Environmental and method — cured by judgement and supervision.
  • Model — the equation's own error. It does not shrink by measuring more trees with it; only a better model reduces it.
  • Measurement propagating into the model — errors in d and h enter BAH before the model sees them, and d enters squared.

Ten mistakes that cost marks and money

  1. Using Smalian on a long unsectioned stem.
  2. Using Huber on stacked logs, where the middle is buried.
  3. Mixing a quarter-girth figure with a true-volume figure.
  4. Applying a conifer form factor to a broadleaf.
  5. Applying a species mean form factor to one valuable stem and quoting it as exact.
  6. Extrapolating beyond the range of a volume table or equation.
  7. Omitting the spline term from Model 7 or 16.
  8. Adding breast height after halving the total height when finding the AFQ point.
  9. Comparing merchantable volume with total volume and calling the difference error.
  10. Reporting a volume without the merchantability standard that produced it.

Section 10Field practical

Use the printed field sheet and the volume calculator.

  1. Before felling, record DBH over bark at the prescribed reference height, from the uphill side, and total height by clinometer from a measured horizontal distance.
  2. After felling, measure total length from the cut face to the tip.
  3. Mark out 2.0 m sections from the base; measure the mid-diameter over bark of each and bark thickness at two points.
  4. Compute each section by Huber's formula and sum for the sectional stem volume; scale the terminal section as a cone.
  5. Compute the same tree by Model 16, and again as V = S·h·f from the published form factor.
  6. Tabulate absolute and percentage differences against the sectional volume, and separate definitional difference from model and measurement error.
  7. State whether the tree lies inside the equation's calibration range.
Safety

Felling is carried out only by the authorised crew. Students remain at a minimum of two tree lengths until the tree is on the ground and the crew signals that it is safe to approach.

Section 11Practice questions

  1. Name the four solids of revolution, give the exponent r for each, and say where each appears in a real stem.
  2. A felled hemlock has DBH 38 cm, total height 25 m, and a sectional volume of 1.42 m³. Calculate its form factor and comment on it against the published mean for the species.
  3. Why is Newton's formula exact for all four solids while Smalian's and Huber's are not?
  4. A 4.00 m log measures 52 cm at the thick end, 45 cm at the middle and 38 cm at the thin end. Compute the volume by all three formulae and rank them.
  5. A log 5.00 m long has a mid-girth of 140 cm. Compute the true volume and the quarter-girth volume, and verify the ratio.
  6. Explain why the absolute form quotient is always lower than the simple form quotient for the same tree.
  7. Using the table in Section 7, estimate the volume of a tree with DBH 25 cm and height 20 m.
  8. A blue pine has DBH 46 cm and height 24 m. Estimate its merchantable volume by Model 16. (The spline arithmetic is set out in the handbook.)
  9. Model 16 gives 1.17 m³ for a tree whose sectional stem volume is 1.35 m³. A student reports a “13 % error”. What is wrong with that statement?
  10. Why is the geographic composition of the fitting sample — 214 of 249 trees from three western dzongkhags — worth stating whenever the model is used elsewhere?

Worked solutions to questions 2, 4, 5, 7 and 8 are in the calculation handbook.

Section 12Summary

  • Four solids: cylinder, paraboloid, cone, neiloid — r = 0, 1, 2, 3.
  • f = V / (S h). Conifers about 0.48–0.55 in Bhutan; oaks much lower.
  • Smalian over-estimates, Huber under-estimates by about half as much, Newton is exact for all four solids.
  • QGV/V = π/4 = 0.7854 — a systematic 21.5 % under-measurement, by convention.
  • A volume table gives the average, not the volume of one tree. Interpolate inside it; never extrapolate.
  • Model 16 predicts merchantable volume to a 10 cm top for blue pine only, fitted on 249 trees, 214 of them from Thimphu, Paro and Haa.
  • Random errors may partly offset; shared model bias, site effects, species differences, measurement bias and extrapolation error do not.
  • Before comparing two volume figures, ask what each of them includes.
