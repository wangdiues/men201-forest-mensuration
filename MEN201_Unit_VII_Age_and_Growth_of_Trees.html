Objectives · what knowing the age lets you do

Age turns a measurement into a rate

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit VII

A tree's size tells you nothing about its productivity until you know how long it took to get there. Two 40 cm trees, one aged 30 years and one aged 120, are entirely different propositions.

1
Time to produce volume and biomass

The productivity question. A site growing 300 m3/ha in 40 years is far more productive than one taking 120 years to do the same.

2
The rate of growth

Size divided by time. Everything in Sections B, C and D of this unit is one way or another of expressing this single quantity.

3
Comparing financial results

Age fixes the timing of costs and revenues. A financial comparison also needs prices, costs, risk and a discount rate.

Rotation age

When to harvest a plantation. The m.a.i culmination in Section C is one benchmark; objectives and the plan decide.

Annual allowable cut

How much may be felled each year without depleting the forest — an increment figure.

Site quality

Height at a stated age. Without the age, top height means nothing at all.

Write this down and keep it: age converts a measurement into a rate. Diameter is a measurement; diameter per year is a rate. Only rates can be compared between sites, species and treatments.

Section A · The problem before the methods

Why the age of a tree is hard to know

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit VII
Nobody was there

The germination year is rarely recorded. A plantation may have a planting year; natural regeneration may have only a felling date, or nothing.

The evidence is inside

Rings record age where they are annual, distinct and cross-dated — but reading them means felling or boring. Neither is possible on every tree.

"Age" needs a datum

Biological age runs from germination; planting age from planting out. A seeding felling is only a proxy. The difference is systematic.

The nursery offset students forget

A plantation record gives the planting year, so it gives planting age. Seedlings spend time in the nursery first, often one to three years. A stand planted in 2000 may have germinated in 1998, so its biological age is two years more than its planting age, for every tree, always in the same direction.

Which age does a ring count give?

Rings counted at a stump or at breast height give the age at that height, not from germination. The years the tree spent growing up to that height must be added back — see slide 14.

The professional habit

State the datum. An age figure without saying what it is measured from is as incomplete as a volume figure without saying over or under bark — the same discipline from Unit V, applied to a different quantity.

Section A · Method 1

Age from existing records

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit VII
Plantation age  =  year of determining the age  −  year of sowing or planting
Natural regeneration age  ≈  year of determining the age  −  year of the seeding felling · a proxy, not the germination year
What a seeding felling is

In the shelterwood system, the cut that opens the canopy so that seed can fall and germinate. It is the nearest thing natural regeneration has to a planting date, but germination follows over later years, so it marks a start, not a birthday.

When it does work

In a well-documented plantation of one planting year, with beating up recorded, this can be the most reliable evidence of planting age — at almost no cost.

Beyond documented plantations, records are weak evidence of a tree's age, for three reasons.

1
Records are of the crop

The plan says "compartment 7, planted 1995". It does not say when the particular tree in front of you germinated.

2
Beating up

Failed patches are replanted a year or two later. Those replacements carry the compartment's planting year in the record and are younger in fact.

3
Regeneration is not simultaneous

Recruitment can spread over years or decades. The source's illustration is up to 40 years: a local figure, not a general one.

Let this land

A stand recorded as a single age may in truth span many years of germination. Check the span locally before you quote one.

Section A · Method 3

Age from whorls of branches and annual shoots

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit VII

Many conifers produce exactly one whorl of branches a year. Count the whorls, and you have counted the years.

Age  =  number of whorls  +  years for the part not visible the same formula serves species showing annual shoot marks
The whole difficulty is the second term

The lowest whorls are shed as the crown lifts and their scars grow over, so a ground count always undercounts. The correction estimates the years taken to reach the lowest whorl still visible.

Worked illustration
Whorls counted
14
Lowest visible whorl
1.2 m above ground
Years to reach 1.2 m
≈ 3 (from local experience)
Age
14 + 3 = ≈ 17 years
Where it fails

Broadleaves, which do not whorl · conifers on poor sites that miss a year · any tree browsed or with a broken leader, which makes a false or doubled whorl.

These methods give only an approximate idea of the age of a tree.

Section A · Method 4

Age from periodic measurements

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit VII

The tree is measured three times at equal intervals. No rings are needed: the age comes from a growth model, not from an observation.

Notation
d1the initial diameter
d2, d3diameters at the next two measurements
P1, P2growth per unit diameter per year, first and second period
Tlength of each period, in years
P1 = d2 − d1d1 T
P2 = d3 − d2d2 T

proportional growth rates per year — not percentages

Age  =  1P1 S
S  =  log P1 − log P2log d2 − log d1 any log base, used throughout: the base cancels in the ratio
The idea worth understanding

S measures how fast the proportional growth rate falls as diameter rises. Assume it fell the same way all through the tree's life, and 1/(P1S) runs that decay backwards to a seedling.

Meaningful only if

intervals are equal · diameters and growth rates are positive · d2 ≠ d1 · S > 0 · no suppression or release has broken the pattern.

Why S came out exactly 1

P1/P2 = 0.02 × 55 = 1.1, and d2/d1 = 33/30 = 1.1. Equal absolute increments always give S = 1, so age = d1 ÷ annual increment = 30 ÷ 0.6 = 50: a constant-rate extrapolation.

What this number is

A model-based estimate. Three recent diameters cannot show early suppression or release, and either would break the backward extrapolation.

Section A · The ring-based method, when it is available

Age of a felled tree · counting the rings

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit VII
How a ring forms

In species with a distinct annual growth cycle the cambium lays down one ring a year: pale, wide-celled early wood in the spring flush, then dense, thick-walled late wood as growth slows. The visible boundary between one year's late wood and the next year's early wood is the ring.

Total age  =  rings counted  +  years to reach the height of the cut
The correction students forget · illustrative ranges
At a 0.3 m stumpadd roughly 2–4 years
At breast height, 1.3 m hereadd roughly 3–8 years
Suppressed under shademay be 15 years to reach 1.3 m
It is a local number, not a constant

Felling or boring a few small trees of known height gives the correction. Quote the source of the figure: ages from ring counts without the correction stated are incomplete. It can be biased as well as uncertain.

Section A · Reading the rings without felling the tree

The increment borer

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit VII
Count all the rings

gives the age at breast height — add the years taken to reach 1.3 m

Measure n complete increments

w is the summed radial width of the outer n annual increments: the radial growth of the last n years

Double it

2w estimates the diameter growth of the last n years, if that radius is representative

Say this three times · a core is a radius, not a diameter

Under bark, d0 = dn − 2w, assuming a round stem with even growth. An eccentric stem needs representative radii: more than one core. Count increments, the spaces between ring boundaries, not boundaries.

Bore at breast height, on the uphill side, perpendicular to the stem and aimed at the pith — a core that misses the pith undercounts. Bore sample trees in permanent plots, not every tree: the wound is small, but in some species it is an infection court.

Section A · The limits of ring counting

When rings lie

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit VII
Course image · CC BY-SA 4.0

A ring count is reliable only where rings are annual, distinct and cross-dated. Four things go wrong, and each has a distinct cause and a distinct effect.

1
False rings

A drought or a defoliation part way through the season stops growth; when conditions improve the cambium restarts and lays a band that looks like late wood in mid-year.

Overestimates the age. A false ring usually fades out round the circumference instead of closing cleanly.

2
Missing rings

In a very bad year, or in a heavily suppressed tree, the cambium may not divide at all round part of the stem. The ring is simply not there on that radius.

Underestimates the age. This is why several radii and several trees are cross-dated.

3
Indistinct rings — test each species

Where trees can grow year-round, many form no clear annual rings. Some broadleaves in warmer, wetter forests may be hard to read; annual rings are species- and site-specific.

Validate before counting. Where rings fail, permanent plots and Method 4 are alternatives, with their own assumptions.

4
Multiple rings a year

Some species flush twice in a season and lay two boundaries in one year.

Overestimates the age, and cannot be detected from a single core at all.

The professional response

Never age a stand from one core. Cross-check several against each other and any record, and report an estimate with a stated range. See NIST, How Do You Measure the Age of a Tree?.

The uncomfortable truth

The best method is usually the one your predecessor made possible. Method 4 needs permanent plots established a decade ago. If nobody established them, the option does not exist — whatever the textbook says.

Section B · Definition

What growth is

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit VII

Growth is the elongation and the thickening of roots, stems and branches — and it causes a tree to change in weight, in volume, and in form.

Two tissues, two kinds of growth
Primary meristem
linear growth — elongation
Secondary meristem
(the cambium)
thickening — diameter growth

The primary meristems sit at the tips — the apical bud of the leader, the tip of each branch, the tip of each root. The cambium is a thin sleeve producing new wood and bark between the old wood and the old bark.

The image that settles it for good

Drive a nail into a stem at 1.3 m. Fifty years later it is still at 1.3 m — the stem below does not elongate. But it will be buried, because the stem around it thickens. Every forester has seen a fence wire swallowed by a tree.

Why this matters back in Unit VI

Because the stem does not elongate below the tip, breast height stays breast height. That is what makes the breast-height mark a stable, repeatable point to re-measure decade after decade — provided every crew uses the same reference height, fixed by the governing protocol: 1.37 m in Bhutan’s Second NFI (DoFPS, NFI Volume I (2023)); 1.30 m in many international manuals. This unit’s examples use 1.3 m as a teaching convention.

The age–diameter curve

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration

Built exactly like Unit VI's curve

Bore or fell a sample across the diameter range, count the rings, plot age against diameter, fit a curve. A cheap measurement then stands in, roughly, for a dear one.

Read it as local and uncertain

Here each centimetre costs about 0.9 yr at 20 cm and 1.2 yr at 80 cm, with scatter of about ±6 yr. Nothing says another stand's curve must steepen.

The warning that governs its use

Use it within one species, one site quality, one stand structure. A suppressed 20 cm tree may be eighty years old while a dominant 20 cm tree on the same hectare is twenty-five.

Section B · The "locality factors" the source keeps referring to

What makes one tree grow faster than another

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit VII
1 · Species
The genetic ceiling

A poplar and a yew on one site differ by an order of magnitude. No silviculture closes that gap.

Decided once, at planting — and not revisitable for a rotation.

2 · Site
The ground itself

Soil depth, texture, drainage, nutrients — and aspect.

In Bhutan's terrain a south and a north slope at one elevation are different sites entirely.

3 · Climate
Above all, season length

Rainfall, temperature, and the length of the growing season.

A stand at 500 m and one at 3 000 m are not comparable, whatever the species.

4 · Competition
Density and crown position

The same genes on the same site grow very differently as a dominant and as a suppressed stem.

This is what Unit VI's basal area measures.

5 · Silviculture
What the forester does

Thinning, weeding, drainage, fertilising, pruning.

Notice what they share: every one works by reducing competition or improving the site.

The ordering to take away

Species and climate set the ceiling. Site sets what is achievable on this ground. Competition and silviculture decide how much of that potential is realised — and a forester spends a career on the last two, because the first two cannot be changed.

Section B · The definition to memorise

Increment

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit VII
Increment is the increase in girth, diameter, basal area, height,
volume, quality, price or value of trees or crops during a given period
Stop on the last three words in that list

Increment is not only physical. A stand whose timber has become more valuable per cubic metre has put on increment in value without adding a single cubic metre of wood. That idea underpins the whole of forest economics.

The shape of a lifetime

Typically the rate is slow at first, becomes rapid at a certain age reaching an optimum, then slows again. Cambial growth usually continues while the tree lives, though height growth slows greatly.

Negative increment

With increasing age the forces of decay become more active than the increment. A hollowing, rotting veteran may lose more wood to decay each year than its cambium adds, so its net volume falls: gross growth continues while net increment is negative.

Growth in what?

The age at which the highest rate is reached is different for different parameters — diameter, height, form, volume. A single "growth rate" for a tree is meaningless without saying growth in what.

It is the slope of the last curve

Near zero at first · a peak where the growth curve is steepest · declining as the growth curve flattens.

Why the peaks often come in that order

Basal area goes with d2, so it can keep rising after diameter increment falls. Volume needs basal area and height. Illustrative, not a law for every tree.

The discrepancy did not grow with the period. The growth did.

p.a.i  =  size at the end  −  size at the beginningnumber of years in the period
A general principle worth naming

When the signal is small, lengthen the period. Growth is a difference of two readings, so it carries both errors: about √2 × one reading's, if independent.

And the price you pay for it

p.a.i is an average: it tells you nothing about what happened within the period. A stand released by a thinning in year three gives the same p.a.i as one that grew steadily.

Say it precisely

c.a.i is not unmeasurable in principle. It is too small and too variable to recover from ordinary annual tape measurements. It can be estimated with dendrometer bands, tree-ring measurement, repeated high-precision observation, or a fitted growth model.

Too short and measurement error swamps the growth; too long and you cannot see when anything happened. Five to ten years is the usual compromise for permanent sample plots.

The relationship between c.a.i and m.a.i

m.a.i is the expression of average yearly response; c.a.i is the actual response in any one year

c.a.i is above the running average, so it pulls the average up. The m.a.i is rising.

Before it, waiting raises mean annual volume; after it, waiting lowers it. That is about volume only. It is not a rule to fell.

Section C · The explanation, at two levels

Why the curves cross at the culmination

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit VII
Discrete data — a running average
A batting average
  • New average = old + (new score − old) ÷ innings
  • Score above the average: it rises. Below: it falls. Equal: it stays
  • Scores 40, 60, 20 give averages 40, 50, 40. It peaked at innings 2, yet no later score equalled the average

So with annual data, look for the switch: m.a.i rises while c.a.i exceeds last year's m.a.i, and falls once it is below. Exact equality need not occur.

Smooth V(A) — the algebra
Definition
m.a.i = V / A
Derivative
d(V/A)/dA = (A·dV/dA − V) / A2
Rewrite
slope of m.a.i = (dV/dA − V/A) / A
Stationary
dV/dA = V/A, i.e. c.a.i = m.a.i
Maximum
c.a.i − m.a.i changes + to −

Equality alone only finds a stationary point. The change of sign is what makes it a maximum.

Two statements, not one

Smooth model: equality at the stationary point, and a sign change makes it the peak. Discrete series: the average peaks at the switch. Both say the same thing about a running average, but only the smooth model promises an exact crossing.

Section C · Where the theory becomes a decision

Culmination and the rotation

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit VII
Culmination of the m.a.i identifies the biological rotation
for maximum mean annual volume production it identifies one rotation — it is not, by itself, the rotation that will be adopted

Cut every stand at that age and, over the long run, the forest produces the greatest average annual volume it is capable of. That is the classical sustained-yield criterion — one criterion among several.

It maximises volume, not value

A later cut can pay if trees move into a higher-value product class, even past the m.a.i peak.

It ignores the cost of waiting

A financial rotation discounts revenues and costs (NPV, LEV). LEV can be shorter or longer than the biological rotation.

It assumes volume is the object

For protection forest, conservation or habitat, the rotation may be far longer — or may not exist at all.

The peak is flat — and that is a gift

11.00 at 35 · 11.13 at 40 · 11.00 at 45. Five years either side costs about 1.1 %.

What actually fixes a rotation in Bhutan

Here age 50 gives 3.8 % less and age 30 gives 5.6 % less than age 40. In a Bhutanese management plan the age finally adopted is set by biodiversity and habitat objectives, watershed protection, livelihood and rural timber demand, regeneration, carbon, cultural values, financial return, and the legal prescription of the approved plan — with the culmination as the biological benchmark against which those choices are judged.

Growth as a physical rate

Unit VII

Increment percentage expresses growth as a proportion of the growing stock already there, per year. The arithmetic is that of an interest rate, but it measures wood, not money.

Both statements are true

In absolute terms the big tree wins ten to one. In proportional terms the pole wins five to one. They answer different questions.

Why the proportional one matters

It compares growth across sizes. It is not a financial return: that needs timber value, prices, quality, costs, risk and the timing of cash flows.

Not a felling rule

Increment percentage generally falls with age and size. Thinning also rests on spacing, crown class, stem quality and objectives.

A warning before the next three slides

Three formulae are in common use. They give three different answers from the same data, and the differences are not small. A percentage quoted without naming the formula is not a usable number.

The three growth percentage formulae

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit VII
Notation — common to all three
S0size at the beginning of the growth period; S0 > 0 Snsize at the end of the growth period nyears in the period

"Size of the parameter" may be diameter, basal area, height or volume. The formulae do not care — do not think of these as volume formulae.

1
Simple interest
P = Sn − S0n S0 × 100

The denominator never changes: every year's growth is measured against the tree as it was at the beginning. No compounding: with positive growth over n ≥ 1 it reads at least as high as compound.

2
Compound interest
P = ( n√( Sn / S0 ) − 1 ) × 100

What constant annual rate, compounded, carries the tree from S0 to Sn in n years? The base grows each year, because the previous increment has been added to it.

3
Pressler's
Pp = Sn − S0Sn + S0 × 200n

Avoids the complication of the compound formula. Dividing by the sum is — bar the factor of 2 — dividing by the average size, so it needs no roots. It lands near compound when the size ratio is modest.

On your calculator

The nth root is the reciprocal power: x(1/n). Find that key now and compute one before the next slide — it is the commonest source of lost marks in this unit.

Pressler's formula and the increment borer of slide 15 come from the same man — Max Robert Pressler, nineteenth-century German forest scientist.

All three formulae need S0, the size n years ago. Nobody measured it, so the tree is made to give up its own record.

d0,ub  =  dn,ub  −  2w w = sum of the radial widths of the outer n complete annual increments

Reporting the estimate honestly

Sources of error and quality control

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit VII
Random error — reduced, not removed
  • Miscounting a ring or two on an unclear core
  • Reading a ring width to the nearest half millimetre
  • Reader-to-reader differences with a tape
  • Not error at all: genuine variation between trees and years is biology. Sample it

More measurements improve precision, though less if they are correlated, as cores from one tree are, and a small sample may not cancel. They do not repair a biased method.

Systematic error — more data will not fix it
  • A core that misses the pith — undercounts on every tree it happens to
  • Forgetting to double the ring width — the commonest error in Section D
  • Ignoring, or misjudging, the years to reach breast height — biases ages
  • The nursery offset in plantation records — understates every age alike
  • Mixed bark bases — distorts growth, in a direction set by the mix
  • Premature rounding — nearly five years on one tree, slide 13
Non-negotiable
habits

Bore several radii and cross-check · record the height at which every count was taken · state the datum for every age · state the formula for every percentage · carry full precision to the end.

Official Bhutan policy context · why this unit exists

Age and growth in Bhutanese forest management

MEN 201 · Forest Mensuration
Unit VII

Increment is the quantity a management plan is built on. Everything in this unit ends in a decision that somebody has to sign.

Allowable harvest

An FMU or community-forest plan sets the annual cut against the increment the forest actually puts on. Harvest held above net increment draws the growing stock down.

Growth monitoring

Permanent sample plots re-measured on a five- to ten-year cycle give the p.a.i that tells a Division whether a stand is responding to management.

Rotation decisions

Culmination of the m.a.i is the biological benchmark. The rotation adopted also answers to biodiversity, watershed, livelihood, carbon, cultural and legal objectives.

Climate resilience

Repeated increment measurement is how a drought year, an insect outbreak or a shift in growing season is detected at all — a declining p.a.i is often the first evidence.

Community forestry

A community-forest management plan must state a sustainable annual harvest of timber, fuelwood and poles. Members' own observation of which stands are growing well is combined with plot re-measurement — local ecological knowledge and formal measurement together, not one instead of the other.

Beyond volume

Growth data also supports carbon reporting, habitat-tree retention, deadwood recruitment, regeneration monitoring and the assessment of forest health. Volume is one variable this unit produces, not its purpose.

It does not show the m.a.i slope today, rule out an earlier peak, or decide whether this compartment should be harvested.

What would decide it

The management plan · biodiversity and habitat · watershed protection · local timber and fuelwood demand · regeneration · carbon · cultural value · and the legal prescription in force.

Where the thread ends

A tape reading in Unit II became a stem, a canopy figure, a height curve, a volume, a hectare and now a rate. Every step inherited the errors of the one before it — which is why the module spent so long on how to make the first measurement properly.

Hypothetical teaching data. Mean diameter increment 0.42 cm a year, applied to every stem and re-expanded; no removals, mortality or ingrowth.

Two conversions that follow
C = πd  ·  d = C / π
dub = dob − 2t
Cub = Cob − 2πt

A diameter crosses bark twice. Subtract 2t, never t.

Crown terms — four names

Crown-base height ground to the base of the live crown · crown length H − crown-base height · crown midpoint height ground to the crown midpoint · live-crown ratio crown length ÷ H. The bare term “crown height” is not used — three incompatible meanings.

Not a symbol, but it belongs here

DBH reference height is protocol-dependent. Bhutan’s Second NFI used 1.37 m; many international manuals use 1.30 m. Unit VII’s examples use 1.3 m. Follow the applicable protocol and record the height used.
