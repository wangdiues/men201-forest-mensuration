Section 1Learning outcomes

  1. Determine the age of a standing tree four different ways, and of a felled tree by ring counting, evaluating the reliability of each.
  2. Calculate the age of a tree from periodic diameter measurements.
  3. Define increment and distinguish current annual, periodic annual, total and mean annual increment.
  4. Locate the culmination of the mean annual increment from a yield series, and state precisely what it does and does not determine.
  5. Compute increment percentage by three formulae and explain why they differ.
  6. Recover a past diameter from an increment core.
The sentence the unit is built on

Age converts a measurement into a rate. Everything in Units II to VI told you how much is there; this unit tells you how fast it is arriving.

Section 2Determining age

Age is harder to know than it looks, and “age” itself needs defining: age from germination? From planting out of the nursery? From the year the seeding felling was done? These differ, sometimes by several years.

Method 1 · existing records

Plantation journals, working plans, compartment histories. Exact where they exist and are trustworthy; useless for natural forest. The method that most often turns out to be wrong in a way nobody notices — always cross-check a record against the tree.

Method 2 · general appearance

Bark character, branching habit, crown form. Cannot be examined and cannot be dispensed with: it is the method that tells an experienced forester the record is wrong. Judgement is the cross-check on the arithmetic.

A caution on bark

Bark character must be learned species by species. Do not generalise a bark description from one species to another.

Method 3 · whorls of branches and annual shoots

Many conifers produce exactly one whorl of branches a year from a single terminal bud. Count the whorls, and you have counted the years.

Age = number of whorls + years for the part not visible

The whole difficulty is the second term. The lowest whorls are shed by natural pruning as the crown lifts and their scars grow over, so a ground count always undercounts. The correction estimates the years taken to reach the lowest whorl still visible.

Worked example 1 · whorl count
Whorls counted14
Lowest visible whorl1.2 m above ground
Years to reach 1.2 m≈ 3 (from local experience)
Age ≈ 17 years

Where it fails: broadleaves, which do not whorl; conifers on poor sites that miss a year; and any tree browsed or with a broken leader, which produces a false or doubled whorl.

Method 4 · periodic measurements

The tree is measured three times at equal intervals. No rings are counted and no judgement is used — which is why this method also works on trees that have no annual rings, the Bhutanese problem in the wet subtropical broadleaf forests of the southern foothills.

SymbolMeaning
d₁the initial diameter
d₂, d₃diameters at the subsequent periodic measurements
P₁growth per unit diameter per year, first period
P₂growth per unit diameter per year, second period
Tlength of each period, in years
P₁ = (d₂ − d₁) ÷ (d₁ × T)   ·   P₂ = (d₃ − d₂) ÷ (d₂ × T) a proportional growth rate per year — not a percentage
S = (log P₁ − log P₂) ÷ (log d₂ − log d₁)   ·   Age = 1 ÷ (P₁ S)
The idea worth understanding

As a tree gets bigger its proportional growth rate falls. S measures how fast that rate falls as diameter rises. Once you know how quickly the rate is decaying you can run the decay backwards to find how long the tree has been growing — which is exactly what 1/(P₁S) does.

On the logarithms: any base will do provided you use the same base throughout, because the base cancels in a ratio.

Worked example 2 · age, and the rounding trap

A tree measured in 1970, 1975 and 1980 with diameters 30, 33 and 36 cm; each period 5 years.

P₁3 ÷ (30 × 5) = 3/150 = 0.020000
P₂3 ÷ (33 × 5) = 3/165 = 0.018182  not 0.018
log P₁ − log P₂−1.698970 − (−1.740363) = 0.041393
log d₂ − log d₁1.518514 − 1.477121 = 0.041393
S0.041393 ÷ 0.041393 = 1.000000 exactly
Age at 19701 ÷ (0.02 × 1.000) = 50 years
Age at 198050 + 10 = 60 years
60 years — computed at full precision

Why S came out exactly 1. P₁/P₂ = 0.02 ÷ 0.018182 = 1.1 exactly, and d₂/d₁ = 33/33 … 33 ÷ 30 = 1.1 exactly. The ratio of two identical logarithms is 1 in any base. The example was built that way.

The rounding trap. Round P₂ to 0.018 and S becomes 1.105, giving 45 years at 1970 and 55 at 1980. A five-year difference — nine per cent — produced entirely by rounding one intermediate value by one per cent. For this module the correct answer is 60; 55 is taught only to demonstrate the trap.

A general property worth noticing

Whenever the two periodic increments are equal in absolute terms, P₁/P₂ = d₂/d₁ identically, so S = 1 for any such data set and the age reduces to d₁ divided by the annual diameter increment.

Method 5 · counting the rings of a felled tree

Exact where rings are distinct — and unavailable on a standing tree you are not felling.

Rings at a stump give the age at that height

Not from germination. The years the tree spent growing up to that height must be added back: roughly 3–8 years to reach breast height on a good site, and as much as 15 years for a stem suppressed under shade.

The increment borer

Extracts a core from bark to pith on a standing tree. The rings on the core are counted, giving the age at the height bored — corrected as above.

When rings lie

False rings — a drought or defoliation mid-season produces a band that looks annual. Missing rings — a severely suppressed tree may add no measurable wood in a bad year. Indistinct rings — this is the Bhutanese problem: in the wet subtropical broadleaf forests of the southern foothills there is no strong dry season to arrest growth, so many species form no clear annual boundary at all. Ring counting there is unreliable to useless, which is exactly why the periodic measurement formula of Method 4 matters.

A core is a radius

The rings record growth on one side only. Diameter grows by twice the ring width. This catches people out repeatedly.

Section 3Growth and increment

Increment is the increase in girth, diameter, basal area, height,
volume, quality, price or value of trees or crops during a given period
Stop on the last three words in that list

Increment is not only physical. A stand whose timber has become more valuable per cubic metre has put on increment in value without adding a single cubic metre of wood. That idea underpins the whole of forest economics.

The shape of a lifetime. The rate is slow at first, becomes rapid at a certain age reaching an optimum, then slows again — but it never stops until the tree dies.

Negative increment. With increasing age the forces of decay become more active than the increment. A hollowing, rotting veteran may lose more wood to decay each year than its cambium adds, so its net volume falls. It is still growing — and still losing ground. This is why a working plan removes over-mature trees rather than leaving them to grow.

Growth in what? The age at which the highest rate is reached is different for different parameters — diameter, height, form, volume. A single “growth rate” for a tree is meaningless without saying growth in what.

Why breast height stays breast height

A stem does not elongate below the tip. Drive a nail into a stem at breast height and fifty years later it is still at the same height — but buried, because the stem around it has thickened. That is what makes the breast-height mark a stable, repeatable point to re-measure decade after decade, provided every crew uses the same reference height.

Section 4The four classes of increment

Increment — in diameter, height or volume alike — is classified only by the period to which it relates. Same tree, same growth, four different periods of accounting.

ClassPeriodWhat you must be able to say
1c.a.i current annual incrementA single year Rarely recoverable from ordinary annual tape measurements — the growth is smaller than the measurement error, so p.a.i is used instead. It can be estimated from dendrometers, tree rings or a growth model
2p.a.i periodic annual incrementAny short period An average annual increment over that period — measure at each end, subtract, divide by the years
3T.I total incrementOrigin to the age of cutting The sum of the c.a.i, and therefore it represents the volume of the tree or crop. Everything a tree contains, it grew
4m.a.i mean annual incrementOrigin to a given age Total increment ÷ that age. For part of the age it is the periodic m.a.i; at the rotation age it is the final m.a.i

Why c.a.i is replaced by p.a.i

Say it precisely

c.a.i is not unmeasurable in principle. It is too small and too variable to recover from ordinary annual tape measurements. A blue pine adds about 4 mm of diameter in a good year; two people reading a girth tape on the same stem commonly differ by 2–3 mm of diameter. The signal is the same size as the noise.

Widen the window to ten years and the same tree adds 40 mm, while the measurement error is still 2–3 mm — now more than ten to one. That is the entire argument for periodic annual increment, and it generalises: when the signal is small, lengthen the period.

p.a.i = (size at the end − size at the beginning) ÷ number of years in the period

And the price you pay. p.a.i is an average: it tells you nothing about what happened within the period. A stand released by a thinning in year three gives the same p.a.i as one that grew steadily. Five to ten years is the usual compromise for permanent sample plots.

Worked example 3 · p.a.i and m.a.i from a permanent plot

A blue pine plot measured in 2015 at 148 m³/ha and in 2025 at 226 m³/ha; planted in 1985.

p.a.i(226 − 148) ÷ 10 = 7.8 m³/ha/yr
Age in 202540 years
m.a.i226 ÷ 40 = 5.65 m³/ha/yr
p.a.i 7.8 > m.a.i 5.65 — the m.a.i is still rising

Note that m.a.i needs the age, which the two measurements alone do not give. m.a.i is measured from origin, not from the last measurement.

Section 5Culmination and rotation

Plotted against age, the c.a.i and m.a.i curves meet exactly twice: once at the end of the first year, and once at the culmination of the m.a.i. Most students remember only the second.

Why they cross exactly at the culmination

A running average rises while the current value is above it and falls while the current value is below it. It is therefore stationary — at its maximum — exactly where the current value equals it. No calculus needed: a new value above the average lifts it, below it lowers it, equal leaves it still.

Culmination of the m.a.i identifies the biological rotation
for maximum mean annual volume production it identifies one rotation — it is not, by itself, the rotation that will be adopted
What p.a.i > m.a.i does and does not tell you

It tells you mean annual volume production is still increasing under the assumed yield sequence, so the m.a.i has not yet culminated. It does not by itself determine whether a stand should be harvested.

What actually fixes a rotation

  • It maximises volume, not value. Large trees are worth more per m³, so the financial rotation is usually longer.
  • It ignores the cost of waiting. Money tied up for forty years must earn its keep, so the economic rotation is usually shorter.
  • It assumes volume is the object. For protection forest, conservation or habitat the rotation may be far longer — or may not exist at all.
In a Bhutanese management plan

The age finally adopted answers to biodiversity and habitat objectives, watershed protection, livelihood and rural timber demand, regeneration, carbon, cultural values, financial return, and the legal prescription of the approved plan — with the culmination as the biological benchmark against which those choices are judged.

The peak is flat — and that is a gift

11.00 at age 35, 11.13 at 40, 11.00 at 45. Ten years either side of the culmination costs about 1 % of production. The rotation can therefore be adjusted by a decade for markets, labour or road access without material loss. The optimum is a broad plateau, not a knife edge.

Section 6Increment percentage

Growth expressed as a rate of return on the growing stock — which is what makes it comparable with a bank rate.

SymbolMeaning
Sosize of the parameter at the beginning of the growth period
Snsize at the end of the period
nnumber of units of time in the period

The parameter may be diameter, basal area, height or volume. The formulae do not care — they are not volume formulae.

1 · Simple interest   P = (Sn − So) ÷ (n So) × 100
2 · Compound interest   P = [(Sn/So)1/n − 1] × 100
3 · Pressler's   Pp = (Sn − So) ÷ (Sn + So) × 200/n

Recovering the past diameter by boring

So = Sn − 2 × (width of the outermost n rings)
Worked example 4 · recovering the past diameter
Present diameter under bark65 cm
Width of 10 outer rings9.5 cm  (a radius)
Diameter growth in 10 yr9.5 × 2 = 19 cm
Diameter 10 years ago65 − 19 = 46 cm

9.5 cm over 10 rings is 9.5 mm of radial growth a year — rapid, a well-grown tree on a good site, not a typical rate.

Worked example 5 · one tree, three formulae

So = 46 cm, Sn = 65 cm, n = 10 years.

Simple(65 − 46) ÷ (10 × 46) × 100 = 19/460 × 100 = 4.13 %
Compound[(65/46)1/10 − 1] × 100 = 3.52 %
Pressler19/111 × 200/10 = 19/111 × 20 = 3.42 %
Simple > compound > Pressler — always, in that order

Why simple always reads highest. It divides by the original 46 for all ten years. Compound divides by a base that grows each year, and Pressler divides by the mean of the two ends. The discrepancy grows with n: over one or two years the three converge and it hardly matters; over ten years simple overstates by about a fifth.

Which to use

Simple — short periods and rough work. Compound — any financial comparison, because it is the only one comparable with an interest rate. Pressler — avoids the complication of the compound calculation while staying close to it.

Units always cancel: every term is a ratio, so the answer is the same in cubic feet or cubic metres. If a growth-percentage answer depends on the units you worked in, you have made an arithmetic error.

Section 7Error and quality control

  • Random — reading the tape, judging a ring boundary, small differences between observers. Averages out with more measurements.
  • Systematic — a stretched tape, a crew measuring at a slightly different height at each re-measurement, consistently counting a false ring. Does not average out, and no amount of extra measurement will fix it.

For increment work specifically:

  • Re-measure at exactly the same point. Mark it; on a permanent plot record the height.
  • Use the same instrument, or calibrate the new one against the old.
  • Carry full precision through the calculation and round once, at the end — Worked example 2 shows a five-year error from rounding in the third decimal place.
  • Record the date of each re-measurement, not just the year.

Section 8In Bhutanese forestry

Increment is the quantity a management plan is built on. Everything in this unit ends in a decision that somebody has to sign.

  • Allowable harvest. An FMU or community-forest plan sets the annual cut against the increment the forest actually puts on. Harvest above increment is depletion, whatever the plan says.
  • Growth monitoring. Permanent sample plots re-measured on a five- to ten-year cycle give the p.a.i that tells a Division whether a stand is responding to management.
  • Rotation decisions. Culmination of the m.a.i is the biological benchmark; the rotation adopted also answers to biodiversity, watershed, livelihood, carbon, cultural and legal objectives.
  • Climate resilience. Repeated increment measurement is how a drought year, an insect outbreak or a shift in growing season is detected at all — a declining p.a.i is often the first evidence.
Community forestry

A community-forest management plan must state a sustainable annual harvest of timber, fuelwood and poles. Members' own observation of which stands are growing well is combined with plot re-measurement — local ecological knowledge and formal measurement together, not one instead of the other.

Section 9Practice questions

  1. From a yield series, tabulate the m.a.i and p.a.i, plot both, locate the culmination and state the rotation of maximum volume production. Show all working.
  2. Describe the four methods of determining the age of a standing tree, and evaluate the reliability of each.
  3. A tree measured in 2000, 2005 and 2010 had diameters 25, 28 and 31 cm. Determine its age in 2010, and explain why S takes the value it does for this data set.
  4. Explain, with a labelled sketch, the relationship between c.a.i and m.a.i, and state how many times the curves meet and why.
  5. A core shows 7.2 cm across the outermost 10 rings; present diameter under bark is 58 cm. Compute the growth percentage by all three formulae and comment on the ordering.
  6. A manager's stand has culminated in m.a.i but timber prices are expected to rise sharply in five years. Should the stand be cut now? Justify.
  7. Prove, by any method you choose, that the m.a.i culminates exactly where the c.a.i crosses it.
  8. Distinguish random from systematic error in age and growth estimation, giving two field examples of each and stating how you would control them.
  9. A stand's p.a.i is 12 and its m.a.i is 9 m³/ha/yr. Has the m.a.i culminated? What does that tell you — and what does it not tell you?
  10. Why can m.a.i never be negative while c.a.i can?

Worked solutions to questions 3, 5 and 7 are in the calculation handbook.

Section 10Summary

  • Five ways to age a tree: records, appearance, whorls, periodic measurements, ring counting. Rings at a stump give age at that height — add the years to reach it.
  • P = (D₂−D₁)/(D₁T); S = (logP₁−logP₂)/(logd₂−logd₁); Age = 1/(P₁S).
  • Four classes of increment, distinguished only by the period: c.a.i, p.a.i, T.I, m.a.i.
  • c.a.i is replaced by p.a.i because one year's growth is smaller than the measurement error — not because it is unmeasurable in principle.
  • The curves meet twice; the second meeting is the culmination of the m.a.i, which identifies the biological rotation and nothing more.
  • Three growth percentages: simple > compound > Pressler, and the gap grows with n. Use compound for any financial comparison.
  • A core is a radius: diameter grows by twice the ring width.
  • Age converts a measurement into a rate.
